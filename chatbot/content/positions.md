## Labor and wages

### Paid family and medical leave
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /wages-labor -->
<!-- aliases: paid family leave; paid leave; family and medical leave; family medical leave -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports establishing a state paid family and medical leave insurance program. In 2026 it supported HB2360. The core argument is that no working family should have to choose between a paycheck and caring for ʻohana. Roughly 90 percent of the public approves, and California's program, in place for more than 20 years, shows that mothers retain higher incomes and rely less on SNAP. Hawaiʻi Appleseed asks lawmakers to preserve the social-insurance model, with shared employee and employer contributions.

### Pay transparency and wage equity
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /wages-labor -->
<!-- aliases: pay transparency; pay equity; equal pay; salary transparency; wage transparency -->
<!-- graphic: pay-gap -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports closing the small-employer carve-out in pay transparency law, and in 2026 supported HB2367, which would eliminate the exemption for employers with fewer than 50 employees. Hawaiʻi women earn between 82.4 and 87 percent of what men earn, backsliding from 92.8 percent in 2014. With equal pay, overall poverty would fall from 5.4 percent to 2.5 percent, and single-mother poverty from 21.3 percent to 10.7 percent. Transparency is the precondition for accountability, so Hawaiʻi Appleseed asks that pay transparency apply to all employers.

### Minimum wage
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /our-history -->
<!-- research: high-cost-low-wages, policy-in-perspective-2022 -->
<!-- aliases: minimum wage; minimum wage indexing; indexing the minimum wage; indexed minimum wage; living wage; minimum wage inflation; indexing the minimum wage to inflation; minimum wage cost of living -->
<!-- graphic: minimum-wage -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed advocated for the 2022 law that phases the minimum wage up from $10.10 to $18 an hour, which is estimated to deliver $1 billion in additional wages each year to more than 200,000 workers. The Issues page notes that the floor still falls short of basic costs once illness or caregiving forces a missed paycheck. It also supports indexing the minimum wage: its 2023 report "The High Cost of Low Wages" argues that tying the wage to inflation or the consumer price index avoids the stagnation that follows each legislative increase, and "Policy in Perspective 2024" lists an indexed minimum wage that adjusts automatically to inflation among the measures that would help working families afford Hawaiʻi.

### Health coverage continuity
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /issues -->
<!-- aliases: bridge coverage; medicaid cuts; health coverage; medicaid churn; medicaid coverage; bridge coverage for medicaid; medicaid bridge coverage; state bridge coverage -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports state bridge coverage as federal Medicaid is cut, and in 2026 supported HB1546. Federal cuts cause churn: eligible people lose coverage to paperwork, not because they are ineligible. The state would need to bridge coverage for undocumented, seasonal, and independent workers. A University of Hawaiʻi survey found 19 percent of residents carry medical debt of $500 or more and 20 percent delay care. Hawaiʻi Appleseed asks that eligibility depend on circumstance, not status.

## Taxes and budget

### Capital gains tax
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: closing-capital-gains-loophole, concentrating-wealth, fair-tax-code-thriving-hawaii -->
<!-- aliases: capital gains; capital gains tax; tax on investment income; taxing investment income -->
<!-- graphic: capital-gains-rates -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed supports raising the capital gains rate, with the ultimate goal of equalizing it with the ordinary income rate of 11 percent. In 2026 it supported HB1850, a raise to 9 percent as a step toward equalization. Hawaiʻi taxes capital gains at 7.25 percent versus 11 percent for ordinary income, a loophole that benefits the wealthy. About 70 percent of Hawaiʻi capital gains go to filers with more than $400,000 in income, and the rate mainly affects those above $783,000. The raise would generate about $55 million. 2026 outcome: the capital gains proposals did not pass. HB1850 stalled in the Senate Committee on Ways & Means, and Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that bold proposals to close the capital gains loophole did not cross the finish line.

### Taxing real estate investment trusts (REITs)
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: fair-tax-code-thriving-hawaii, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: reit; reits; real estate investment trust; real estate investment trusts; reit loophole -->
<!-- graphic: reit-stats -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed supports actually taxing REITs rather than commissioning another study. In 2026 it supported SB2362 but asked that it be amended from a study to a tax. Hawaiʻi exempts REIT income even though REITs hold more than $17 billion in Hawaiʻi assets; in 2014, REITs reported $900 million in Hawaiʻi income and paid $0 in state corporate income tax. 2026 outcome: Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that proposals to close corporate tax loopholes did not cross the finish line, and SB2362 had been changed from a tax to a study.

### Millionaire's income surcharge
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: fair-tax-code-thriving-hawaii, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: millionaire tax; millionaires tax; millionaire surcharge; tax on millionaires; income surcharge; millionaires income tax -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports a 2 percent surcharge on income above $1 million, and in 2026 supported HB2010. It would generate roughly $71 million a year to help fund Medicaid, with the top 1 percent paying an additional 0.72 percent of income. Massachusetts adopted a similar surcharge and its wealth continued to grow afterward, which answers the "wealth flight" argument. Hawaiʻi Appleseed asks lawmakers not to water down the threshold or the rate. 2026 outcome: SB3125, signed into law as Act 24 on May 21, 2026, added a 2 percent surcharge on income over $1 million. HB2010 itself was deferred in committee.

### Freezing and adjusting Act 46
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: fair-tax-code-thriving-hawaii, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: act 46; freezing act 46; income tax cuts; 2024 tax cuts; act 46 tax cuts; freezing act 46 tax cuts; rolling back act 46 -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports freezing the 2024 income tax cuts in Act 46 and adjusting the top brackets upward. In 2026 it supported HB2306 and SB3125. The cuts are unevenly distributed and cost the state billions. Freezing the rollout and raising the top five brackets by 0.25 to 1 percentage point would generate about $69 million, 86 percent of it from the top 1 percent. Hawaiʻi Appleseed frames this as a measured fiscal correction, not an attack. 2026 outcome: SB3125 was signed into law as Act 24 on May 21, 2026. It rolled back some of the Act 46 tax cuts for the wealthy, which Policy in Perspective 2026 called a critical victory, but it did not undo them: Act 46 will still reduce state revenue by more than $1 billion a year by 2031. HB2306 did not advance.

### Medical debt forgiveness
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- aliases: medical debt; medical debt relief; medical debt forgiveness; medical debt relief program; buying medical debt -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports state-funded medical debt acquisition and forgiveness, and in 2026 supported SB3025. It would use the nonprofit acquisition model that 27 states and cities now use, forgiving medical debt for households at or below 400 percent of the federal poverty level, or with debt of 5 percent of income or more. Medical debt cascades into delayed care, housing instability, and worse health.

### Conveyance tax modernization
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: preserving-hawaii, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: conveyance tax; conveyance tax reform -->
<!-- graphic: conveyance-allocation -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports a marginal-rate conveyance tax with a progressive rate structure and dedicated revenue, and in 2026 supported HB2049. The conveyance tax has not been updated since 2009. The proposal protects owner-occupied homes under $2.2 million, raises rates on high-value investment properties, and indexes to inflation. Revenue would be dedicated to affordable housing (20 percent), the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands (30 percent), DURF (20 percent), and conservation (5 percent). Hawaiʻi needs more than 50,000 housing units, and Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that nearly 30,000 Native Hawaiian families sit on the DHHL waitlist. Hawaiʻi Appleseed's framing is that those who profit most from our islands also invest most in their future, and that housing should come ahead of handcuffs, contrasting a $300 million prison budget with $185 million for DHHL. 2026 outcome: HB2049 was deferred by the Senate Committee on Ways & Means, and SB3028, which would have given DHHL up to $60 million a year, died in conference committee, according to Policy in Perspective 2026.

### Keiki Credit (state child tax credit)
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: investing-in-our-future-a-keiki-credit-for-hawaii -->
<!-- aliases: keiki credit; child tax credit; state child tax credit; ctc -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed proposes a state Keiki Credit, a child tax credit. Its 2023 report, "Investing in our Future," recommends a larger credit for children under five and a smaller one for children up to 18, and making the credit fully refundable and available regardless of income, because only part of the federal child tax credit is refundable.

## Housing and renters

### Renter protections
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /affordable-housing -->
<!-- research: renters-left-behind-hawaii-state-legislature, understanding-hawaii-landlord-tenant-code, keeping-hawaii-housed -->
<!-- aliases: renter protections; tenant protections; just cause eviction; just cause eviction law; eviction protections; right to counsel for tenants -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed supports a comprehensive package of renter protections. From its 2025 report "Renters Left Behind," the asks are just-cause eviction, a right to legal counsel for tenants in eviction proceedings, 60 days' notice for lease non-renewal and rent increases, and real enforcement mechanisms rather than voluntary compliance. About 38 percent of Hawaiʻi households rent, and Maui and Kauaʻi have among the highest median rents in the country. Renters are routinely sidelined in housing policy despite carrying the highest cost burden. Wins to point to: Act 57 (2021) created mandatory mediation that became a national model, and Act 237 (2022) raised TANF by $500. 2026 outcome: Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that the state missed an opportunity to advance just-cause eviction legislation in 2026.

### Rent control and rental vouchers
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: https://hiappleseed.org/blog/unburden-hawaii-expand-rental-voucher-program -->
<!-- aliases: rent control; rent regulation; rent stabilization; rent caps; rental vouchers; rental voucher program; housing vouchers; rent control vs vouchers -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed argues that rental vouchers, not rent control, are the better way to help rent-burdened families. In a 2026 blog post, "Unburden the burdened," it says price controls treat a symptom, not the disease: they protect current tenants rather than the rent-burdened families still searching for a home, do nothing to increase the housing supply Hawaiʻi needs, and often benefit middle- and high-income households more than the working families they are meant to help. It points to California, where 57 percent of rent-controlled units are occupied by middle- or higher-income renters, and to San Francisco, where rent control reduced the rent-controlled housing stock by 15 percent. It argues that Hawaiʻi should expand its rental voucher program: increase the State Rent Supplement Program amount to more than $500 a month, offer landlords who accept vouchers guaranteed rent payments, free inspections, and home improvement support in every county, and convert Housing Choice Vouchers to project-based vouchers. This is a published argument, not a vote of the board. The renter protections Hawaiʻi Appleseed does support, such as just-cause eviction, are described separately.

### Empty homes tax (Honolulu)
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /affordable-housing -->
<!-- research: empty-homes-tax-honolulu -->
<!-- aliases: empty homes tax; empty home tax; vacant homes tax; vacancy tax; tax on vacant homes; tax on empty homes; eht; vacant properties tax; tax on vacant properties; vacant property tax; tax on vacant homes in honolulu -->
<!-- graphic: eht-revenue -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed recommends that the City & County of Honolulu adopt an empty homes tax to discourage speculative investment and raise revenue for city services. Its 2024 report, "Homes Without Residents," estimates that about 35,000 units sit empty across Honolulu, and that more than six in 10 Honolulu renters spend at least 30 percent of their income on housing. It recommends a flat tax of 3 to 5 percent, modeled on Vancouver's tax, which funds affordable housing; a mandatory annual declaration of occupancy status; limited exemptions (new units until the developer sells them, medical reasons, natural disasters, construction or renovation, and homes rented to long-term tenants); penalties for late or false declarations; and funding for administration. The report estimates that a 3 percent tax would raise about $183.4 million a year and a 5 percent tax about $305.67 million. At the time, the City Council was considering Bill 46 (2024), a 3 percent tax on properties vacant more than six months a year. The bill's final status has not been checked.

### Parking mandates
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /affordable-housing -->
<!-- research: stalled-parking-mandates-housing-costs -->
<!-- aliases: parking mandates; parking minimums; parking maximums; parking requirements; parking reform; unbundling parking -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed's 2025 report "Stalled" recommends limiting how much parking new housing must include: a maximum parking ratio of one space per dwelling unit in transit-oriented development (TOD) areas, and unbundling parking from rent and sale prices so tenants and buyers are not forced to pay for parking they do not need. It argues that parking maximums prevent an oversupply of parking and free up space for housing.

### Housing and family tax credits
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: tax-credits-advance-prosperity, refunding-hawaii, helping-hawaii-families -->
<!-- aliases: earned income tax credit; eitc; working families tax credit; wctc; state eitc; refundable eitc; tax credits -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed supports strengthening the Working Families Tax Credit (WCTC), the federal and state Earned Income Tax Credit, and a state-level child tax credit. In 2022, 15,664 residents left Hawaiʻi because of the cost of living. Refundable tax credits put money in working families' hands for everyday expenses such as food, rent, and medical care. A 2022 report with the Hawaiʻi Children's Action Network, "Refunding Hawaiʻi," argues that the state EITC should be made refundable.

## Food equity

### Universal free school meals
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /food-equity -->
<!-- research: equity-on-the-menu, feed-our-keiki, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: school meals; free school meals; universal school meals; school lunch; free school lunch; school breakfast -->
<!-- graphic: school-meals-cliff -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports universal free school meals. In 2026 it supported HB1779, universal meals by 2030, and HB2296, removing the revenue-requirement obstacle. Its framing is that school meals are essential school supplies. Three in 10 households with children face food insecurity. The means-tested system fails: a family of four earning $47,000 or more does not qualify, even though about $107,000 is needed to cover living costs in Hawaiʻi, and 34 percent of denied students are actually eligible but fall through the paperwork. For some keiki, school meals are the only balanced meals they receive. Hawaiʻi charter schools serve 14 percent more Native Hawaiian students than DOE schools and many incorporate local and canoe crops, so universal coverage matters for cultural reasons too. Hawaiʻi Appleseed opposes phase-ins that delay implementation past 2030. 2026 outcome: Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that lawmakers did not fund an expansion of free school meals to all students by 2030, and HB1779 did not advance past the Senate Committee on Education referral. HB2296 was signed into law (Act 236, July 13, 2026) and lowers the required charge for school meals from half to one-quarter of production cost, which the report called a small but significant win. Free school meals had already been expanded in 2025 under Act 139.

### SNAP access
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /food-equity -->
<!-- research: hawaii-snap, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: snap; food stamps; snap benefits; snap certification; esap -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports extending SNAP certification and adopting the federal ESAP waiver, and in 2026 supported SB3245. More than 160,000 Hawaiʻi residents depend on SNAP. Extending certification from 12 to 24 months, and adopting ESAP (up to 36 months for seniors), reduces churn, where eligible people lose benefits to paperwork. Twenty-three states already use ESAP. 2026 outcome: SB3245 was signed into law as Act 193 on July 7, 2026, according to the Policy in Perspective 2026 scorecard.

## Transportation

### Transit equity and fare-free transit
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /transportation-equity -->
<!-- research: keiki-ride-free, policy-perspective-2026 -->
<!-- aliases: fare free transit; free transit; free buses; free bus; keiki ride free; youth ride free; free public transit; free bus rides; free bus rides for kids; free rides for kids; free transit for youth -->
<!-- graphic: transit-budget -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed supports fare-free transit for keiki and for state employees, and pursuit of universal fare-free transit. In 2026 it supported HB1879 (Keiki Ride Free, $9.87 million a year), SB2993 (state employees), and HB2451 (all users). Removing cost barriers expands youth and disability access. Maui has fare-free service for riders under 24 and Hawaiʻi County is fully fare-free; Washington state offers free transit under 18, and Kansas City saw ridership surge after going fare-free. Sixty-six percent of HDOT's $5.68 billion (FY19–24) went to vehicle capacity and only 1.5 percent to transit alternatives; Keiki Ride Free is 0.87 percent of the five-year budget. The work is tied to Navahine v. HDOT and the 2045 decarbonization mandate. 2026 outcome: Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that Keiki Ride Free (HB1879), subsidized transit for state employees (SB2993), and free transit for all (HB2451) were killed or failed to advance in 2026.

### Pedestrian safety
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /transportation-equity -->
<!-- research: pedestrian-head-start, freedom-to-walk, beyond-the-ticket -->
<!-- aliases: pedestrian safety; pedestrian head start; leading pedestrian intervals; lpi; accessible pedestrian signals -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed strongly supports Leading Pedestrian Intervals (LPIs) and Accessible Pedestrian Signals (APS), and in 2026 supported HB1884 and SB2470 ("Pedestrian Head Start"). A three to seven second head start at intersections measurably saves lives: Seattle saw a 48 percent reduction in turning collisions and a 34 percent reduction in serious or fatal pedestrian injuries, and New York City's 2025 results were a 33 percent injury reduction and a 65 percent fatal reduction. Installation costs hundreds to a few thousand dollars per intersection. The measures disproportionately benefit keiki, kūpuna, people with disabilities, and people who rely on walking and transit. 2026 outcome: Policy in Perspective 2026 reports that Pedestrian Head Start (SB2470) cleared its committee hearings and reached conference, but no conferees were ever assigned and it died in conference committee.

### Decriminalizing jaywalking
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /transportation-equity -->
<!-- research: freedom-to-walk -->
<!-- aliases: jaywalking; decriminalizing jaywalking; jaywalking laws; decriminalize jaywalking; jaywalking tickets -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed recommends decriminalizing jaywalking by amending the state traffic code (HRS §291C) so that pedestrians cannot be stopped, fined, or penalized for acting contrary to the traffic code, along with studying which communities are most affected by jaywalking enforcement and how much public money it uses. Its 2024 report, "Freedom to Walk," says that jaywalking laws and similar pedestrian enforcement have not reduced pedestrian deaths and injuries, and calls for a shift toward safe, accessible pedestrian infrastructure with dedicated funding.

### Traffic enforcement
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /transportation-equity -->
<!-- research: beyond-the-ticket -->
<!-- aliases: traffic enforcement; unarmed traffic enforcement; traffic tickets; traffic fines -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed's 2025 report "Beyond the Ticket" recommends that local law enforcement agencies build unarmed teams to enforce minor traffic violations, because most do not require armed police and the presence of law enforcement can create stressful, hostile environments. It points to Berkeley, California, which approved a plan to have unarmed employees carry out most of its traffic enforcement.

## Measures Hawaiʻi Appleseed opposes

### Over-broad tax cuts
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: policy-in-perspective-2024 -->
<!-- aliases: tax cuts; broad tax cuts; sweeping tax cuts; hb2404 -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed opposes sweeping, untargeted tax cuts that drain the general fund without helping working families. In 2024 it opposed HB2404. Its over-broad cuts created major budget trouble; the benefit skews to higher earners while the revenue loss threatens services that low- and moderate-income families rely on.

### Tobacco and vaping taxes
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: policy-in-perspective-2022 -->
<!-- aliases: vaping tax; vape tax; e-cigarette tax; tobacco tax; tobacco taxes; cigarette tax; taxing vaping; vaping -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed recommends taxing vaping and raising other tobacco taxes. Its 2022 "Policy in Perspective" report says the rise of vaping and e-cigarette use among Hawaiʻi's youth has erased decades of tobacco prevention and control progress, and that with no state tobacco tax on e-cigarette products, these lower-priced alternatives are more appealing to youth even though their use carries health risks. Research shows that higher prices reduce smoking by adults and youth. About 25 states tax e-cigarette products, and the report estimates that a tax at 95 percent of the wholesale price, based on milliliters sold, could raise around $8.2 million. It also recommends raising cigarette taxes, which had not changed in 10 years.

### Estate tax breaks
<!-- status: draft -->
<!-- url: /taxes-budget -->
<!-- research: concentrating-wealth -->
<!-- aliases: estate tax; estate tax breaks; estate tax cuts; estate tax exemption; hb2652; hb2653; inheritance tax -->

Hawaiʻi Appleseed opposes estate tax break bills. In 2024 it called HB2652 and HB2653 trickle-down bills that are bad policy for Hawaiʻi. HB2652 would expand the marital deduction to immediate family members, effectively eliminating the estate tax by letting estates pass to children tax-free; HB2653 would link Hawaiʻi's estate tax exemption to the federal exemption and deduct the value of a family-owned business. Hawaiʻi Appleseed argues that there is no evidence local businesses need them, that they would give millions of dollars in unnecessary tax relief to the wealthiest residents, and that the shortfall would draw funds away from priorities that help working families.
