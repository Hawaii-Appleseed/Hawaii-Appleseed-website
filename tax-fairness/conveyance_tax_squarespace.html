Where the conveyance tax stands

The conveyance tax is paid when real estate changes hands. Its rates were last changed in 2009, and property values in Hawaiʻi have risen considerably since then. Today its revenue goes to the general fund, the Land Conservation Fund and the Rental Housing Revolving Fund. It does not currently include funding for the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands (DHHL).

DHHL’s waitlist for a homestead had 29,589 applicants as of November 2025.

Where the money goes today

The law splits conveyance tax collections three ways. The Rental Housing Revolving Fund gets 50 percent, up to $38 million a year. The Land Conservation Fund gets 10 percent, up to $5.1 million a year. The general fund gets the rest. Hawaiian Home Lands is not currently part of the split.

Because both funds are capped, once collections pass about $76 million a year, every added dollar goes to the general fund.

Rates and allocation: Hawaiʻi Revised Statutes sections 247-2 and 247-7.

Why the tax should be marginal

Today the conveyance tax has price tiers, and the rate for a sale’s tier applies to the entire price. When a sale crosses into the next tier, the higher rate applies to every dollar, not just the dollars above the line. That creates cliffs: an owner-occupied home that sells for $4,000,000 owes $8,005 more in tax than one that sells for $3,999,000. It also gives sellers a reason to price a home just under each line.

A marginal tax works like income tax brackets: each rate applies only to the slice of the price inside its band, so the tax rises smoothly as the price does.

That is what lets the tax ask much more of the most expensive sales while charging typical buyers less. Under the Legislature’s April 2026 draft, buyers who live in the home would owe less than today on every sale up to about $2.25 million, and other residential buyers on every sale up to about $2.07 million.

Draft: SB 3028 SD2 HD2 (April 8, 2026), which died in conference.

What we are asking for

  • Switch to marginal rates, so each rate applies only to the part of the price in its band and there are no cliffs.
  • Ask more of high-value and investment property sales, while protecting local families buying a home to live in.
  • Dedicate the new revenue to DHHL, so the trust can build homes and infrastructure for its beneficiaries.

What it could raise

A House committee estimated what the conveyance tax would bring in if SB 3028 used the rates of HB 2049 HD3: $271 million in total in FY2027, rising to $303 million by FY2031. That is all conveyance tax revenue, not just the increase. It would fill every special fund to its cap, including $60 million a year for DHHL, and send the rest to the general fund.

SB 3028 SD2 HD2, the last draft before the bill died in conference, would send 30 percent of collections, up to $60 million a year, to Hawaiian Home Lands, along with dedicated funding for rental housing, urban development, and land conservation.

Estimate: House committee report, SB 3028 SD2 HD1 with the rates of HB 2049 HD3 (2026), FY2027–FY2031; applies to residential property.
