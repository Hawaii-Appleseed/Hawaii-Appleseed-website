Hawaiʻi Appleseed is committed to a more socially and economically just Hawaiʻi, where everyone has genuine 
opportunities to achieve economic security and fulfill their potential. We change systems to address inequity 
and foster greater opportunity by conducting data analysis and research to address income inequality, educating 
policymakers and the public, engaging in collaborative problem solving and coalition building, and advocating 
for policy and systems change.

The work of Hawaiʻi Appleseed is about people. The issues we work on—housing, food, wages, mobility, the state 
budget and taxation, and racial and indigenous equity—are important because they ensure people have access 
to shelter, sustenance, and the means to survive and thrive individually and collectively. Addressing these issues 
requires the knowledge and expertise of the people that have first-hand experience and live with the adverse 
consequences of our flawed systems.

2 • A THRIVING HAWAI‘I

INTRODUCTION: 
A THRIVING HAWAI‘I

HAWAIʻI STANDS AT A CROSSROADS. The path we choose now will define our islands for generations. We can 
either accept a future of diminishing public goods and deepening inequality, or we can choose to build one of 
shared prosperity and collective resilience. 

This choice hinges on a fundamental question of values: who pays for the society we all share? A just future—one 
with quality education, affordable housing, reliable healthcare, and climate-resilient infrastructure—requires a 
fair and adequate investment from all—especially those who have benefitted the most. 

Our current tax code, however, asks less from the wealthy and powerful, leaving working families to shoulder a 

A THRIVING HAWAI‘I • 3

disproportionate burden and starving our communities of the resources needed to thrive. To build the Hawaiʻi 
we believe in, we must reform our tax system to ensure everyone pays their fair share.

This imperative for equitable and sustainable revenue has become urgently practical. Due to the 2025 federal 
budget bill, HR1, Hawaiʻi is facing the loss of hundreds of millions of dollars in federal funding over the next few 
years. This will leave funding gaps throughout the state budget, especially with Medicaid and SNAP. In addition 
to these cuts, the bill provides $491 million in federal tax cuts to Hawaiʻi’s wealthiest residents. 

These federal cuts come on the heels of Hawaiʻi’s historic income tax cuts that were passed in Act 46, Session 
Laws of Hawaiʻi 2024, which will eventually cost the state over $1 billion a year in tax revenue (see Figure 2).

The state has three options to address these issues: 
•  Cut services;
• 
• 

Issue additional general obligation bonds (pass the costs on to future generations); or
Find other sources of revenue to continue supporting the programs and services that many local residents 
depend on. 

For thousands of Hawaiʻi’s residents, Medicaid and SNAP benefits are a matter of survival. We cannot abandon 
our friends, family, and neighbors who need this support now more than ever. Future generations have already 
been asked to shoulder the consequences of many past decisions. This is a problem we can solve now.

Given that Hawaiʻi’s tax system is regressive (meaning lower-income residents pay a higher effective tax rate than 
higher-income residents),1 we recommend legislators balance the state’s upside tax system by asking the wealthy 
and corporations to pay their fair share. 

Doing so is a practical investment in our collective future—a future that should be full of opportunity and success 
for all of Hawaiʻi’s people, not just a privileged few.

PRESSURE ON 
HAWAI‘I’S BUDGET

Act 46 will place the state budget under significant strain in the years to come. That’s on top of the many critical 
needs facing our communities, all of which need funding to address. Hawaiʻi will be forced to make changes 
to the tax code in order to boost revenue to balance the state budget, and this revenue should come only from 
higher-income households and corporations that can afford to pay more.

ACT 46 CONCERNS

WHILE NEARLY ALL HOUSEHOLDS WILL PAY LESS TAXES AS A RESULT, THE 
LARGEST TAX CUTS WILL GO TO HIGHEST-INCOME HOUSEHOLDS.

At the start of 2024, House Bill 2404 was introduced with the aim of helping Hawaiʻi’s residents with the high cost 
of living by giving them a minor tax break. Each year, it would have slightly adjusted income tax brackets, the 
standard deduction, and personal exemptions to keep up with inflation.

On the last day of the 2024 legislative session, HB2404 was overhauled in Conference Committee with no public 
input or oversight. The version that emerged, signed into law as Act 46, makes much larger adjustments to the 
income tax brackets than the version that entered Conference Committee. This means that a larger number of 
high-income households will be taxed at lower rates compared to the original version. 

As an example, the top income bracket for a single filer in 2024 was set at $200,000 and over, with an income tax 
rate of 11 percent. This top 11 percent bracket has already shifted to $325,000 in 2025, and it will rise to $475,000 
in 2029.2

Distribution of Tax Cuts

Overall, Act 46 favors wealthier taxpayers in Hawaiʻi. The top 1 percent of earners will get an average tax break of 
$6,045 in 2025, which will more than double to $12,802 by 2031. 

In contrast, the lowest-earning 20 percent will see a much smaller average cut of $335 in 2025, increasing to just 
$469 by 2031.

However, there is one change in Act 46 that is focused squarely on low- to middle-income people who truly need 
more tax relief: the massive boost to the standard deduction. By 2031, the standard deduction will jump from 
$2,200 to $12,000 for single filers, and from $4,400 to $24,000 for married couples. 

This increased standard deduction helps reduce taxes for lower-income people, as they tend to not itemize their 
deductions in the same manner as the wealthy. This component of Act 46 is worthy of preservation.

Impact on Services

As Act 46 goes into effect, Hawaiʻi's budget will face increasing pressure, especially as mandatory expenses like 
Medicaid, retiree health, pensions, and debt payments continue to rise. These expenses already take up about a 
quarter of the state’s operating budget.5

Without new revenue streams, the state will have to cut programs or stop providing services. This is similar to 
what happened during the Great Recession. At that time, a smaller loss in state revenue still led to major cuts in 
education, health, and social services. Hawaiʻi is still dealing with the consequences of those cuts, with lagging 
educational attainment and worsening homelessness across the state.

PROGRESSIVE 
REVENUE PROPOSALS

There are a number of options to raise revenue for Hawaiʻi without adding a financial burden to low- to middle-
income residents. The most straightforward option would be to freeze the income bracket changes in Act 46, 
which would immediately stop the cost of the tax cuts from increasing further (Recommendation 1). 

However, freezing unnecessary tax cuts for the wealthy still leaves us with a tax system in which large 
corporations the richest among us pay less than their fair share. There are many policies at the state’s disposal 
to correct that. These include: taxing capital gains as regular income, creating special taxes on millionaires and 
wealth proceeds, restructuring the conveyance tax, and taxing real estate investment trusts (Recommendation 2).

RECOMMEMDATION 1: FREEZE ACT 46
POTENTIAL REVENUE SAVED: $296 MILLION PER YEAR

Legislators should freeze the implementation of Act 46’s tax bracket changes. This would keep the income 
brackets at their current amounts, while allowing the standard deduction amounts to increase. 

This single action could save at least $296 million in Tax Year 2027—and it would also stop the costs from 
ballooning to $1.4 billion a year beginning in 2032.9 At the same time, it would continue to provide tax relief to 
low- and middle-income households that typically do not itemize their tax returns.

RECOMMEMDATION 2: SEEK OTHER PROGRESSIVE REVENUE OPPORTUNITIES
POTENTIAL REVENUE RAISED: $335.8–$412 MILLION PER YEAR

Along with freezing Act 46, Hawaiʻi Appleseed has gathered a list of proven revenue options that would have a 
minimal impact on low- to middle-income taxpayers. All together, these options could raise over $400 million 
each year.

OPTION 1: CLOSING THE CAPITAL GAINS TAX LOOPHOLE
Potential Revenue Raised: $44–85 million per year

Capital gains are the profits from investments like stocks, bonds, real estate, and art. In Hawaiʻi, capital gains are 
taxed at a maximum rate of 7.25 percent, which is lower than the upper tax rates for regular income. This creates 
a tax giveaway for the wealthy, who tend to earn a larger portion of their income through capital gains than 
everyone else, and who overwhelmingly hold capital assets.

For example, a high-income person who earns over $325,000 would pay a marginal tax rate of 11 percent on their 
income from a job. This same person would pay a lower rate of 7.25 percent on their passive income from selling 
stocks. About 68 percent of the capital gains in the state go to people earning over $400,000.11

Capital gains should be taxed at the same rate as ordinary income—ensuring that all taxpayers pay the 
appropriate tax rate on their income, no matter how they earn it.

OPTION 3: RESTRUCTURING THE CONVEYANCE TAX
Potential Revenue Raised: $68.5 million per year

The Conveyance Tax is a transfer tax paid on the sale of real estate in Hawaiʻi. The current structure of the tax 
can lead to sudden jumps in the amount owed when moving from one bracket to the next (e.g. from the sale of a 
house valued under $1 million versus a house valued above $1 million).

This tax structure could be improved by switching to a marginal rate, similar to income taxes, where only the 
portion above each price threshold is taxed at higher rates. It could also be improved by increasing the rates on 
luxury homes and second homes.

Restructuring the Conveyance Tax to use marginal rates would actually lower the amount most middle-class 
homeowners would owe upon selling a home. Meanwhile, the owners of high-value homes (many of which live 
out-of-state) would pay more.

Because significant portions of the Conveyance Tax revenue is earmarked for specific funds, much of the 
extra revenue from these changes would go to key priorities like affordable rental housing, transportation 
infrastructure, and the Department of Hawaiian Homelands.

OPTION 4: TAX REAL ESTATE INVESTMENT TRUSTS
Potential Revenue Raised: $60 million+ per year

A real estate investment trust (REIT) is a company that owns income-generating properties, such as hotels, 
malls and office buildings. People can buy shares in a REIT and earn part of its profits. Examples of REIT-
owned properties include Ala Moana Center, International Marketplace, the Hilton Hawaiian Village, and 
Pearlridge Center.

In 2018, Hawaiʻi’s REITs owned more than $17 billion in real estate and earned roughly $1 billion a year. 12 Since 
REITs do not pay corporate income tax on 90 percent of their profits, Hawaiʻi lost at least $60 million in tax 
revenue in 2018. That figure is likely much higher for 2025.13

The majority of REIT shareholders do not live in Hawaiʻi, which means that their income from dividends 
(investments) is not taxed here. As a result, most of the profit earned from commercial properties in Hawaiʻi 
flows out of the state without being taxed.

Closing the REIT tax loophole would ensure these large property owners contribute what they owe to Hawaiʻi’s 
economy. REITs would still keep their federal tax benefits and low local property tax rates, while helping to 
support the local communities their properties depend on.

OPTION 5: WEALTH PROCEEDS TAX
Potential Revenue Raised: $53.9 million (1% rate)14 per year

The Wealth Proceeds Tax is functions like an expanded capital gains tax—but only for people with very high 
incomes. Most of the tax is paid by millionaires. 

This added 1 percent tax rate would only apply to investment income over $200,000 (single) or $250,000 
(married). Only the wealth proceeds above those amounts is taxed. The tax would apply to income from capital 
gains, dividends, interest, certain business profits, rents, royalties, and some annuity payments

Since wealth proceeds are taxed at the federal level, states can “piggyback” on the existing federal form when 
creating their own wealth proceeds tax.

Minnesota successfully passed a wealth proceeds tax in 2023, set at a tax rate of 1 percent.

This tax would add a 2 percent surcharge on any income over $1 million. For example, if someone earns $1.2

Research on Massachusetts’ 2022 millionaire’s tax shows the state has subsequently experienced growth in both

CONCLUSION

The choices before us reflect more than a fiscal equation; they are a statement of our values as a community. We 
can continue down the path of a tax system that asks the most from those with the least, while the wealthiest 
contribute less and our public needs go unmet. Or, we can choose a future of shared investment and shared 
prosperity.

The proposals outlined here—from halting the costly Act 46 tax cuts for upper income households, to enacting 
fair taxes on capital gains, luxury properties, and extreme wealth—provide a clear roadmap. They are practical, 
proven policies that ask those who have benefited the most from our economy and society to finally pay their fair 
share. 

By adopting these measures, we can generate the revenue necessary to protect vital services like Medicaid and 
SNAP, and to invest boldly in housing, education, climate resilience, and the foundations of a thriving Hawaiʻi for 
all. This is how we honor our kuleana to each other and build the resilient, equitable future our islands deserve. 
Let us choose the path of fairness, and fund our shared future together.
