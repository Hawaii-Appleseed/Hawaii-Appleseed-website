BACKGROUND

SIGNALIZED INTERSECTIONS are some of the most dangerous places for people walking and biking in Hawaiʻi.1 
These crossings put high volumes of cars and vulnerable road users in direct conflict, often with tragic results. 
Low-cost solutions like Accessible Pedestrian Signals (APS) and Leading Pedestrian Intervals (LPIs) give people a 
safer head start and make crossings more inclusive for those with disabilities. 

While these tools are standard practice in cities across the globe, they are virtually absent in Hawaiʻi. 
With pedestrian injuries and fatalities on the rise, it’s time for lawmakers to require our state and county 
transportation agencies to act.

ACCESSIBLE PEDESTRIAN SIGNALS (APS)

APS are devices that communicate information 
to blind or low-vision pedestrians about the 
WALK and DON’T WALK intervals at signalized 
intersections in non-visual formats, such as 
audible tones and vibrotactile surfaces. In some 
places, APS also provides audible and Braille 
information about the intersection name and 
crosswalk orientation.2 

These systems are intended to supplement, but 
not entirely replace, traffic cues.3 For example, 
while the APS can indicate when a person can start 
crossing an intersection, the sound of vehicles is 
still important for blind and low-vision pedestrians 
to know when vehicles are approaching. 

Research shows that APS help pedestrians more 
accurately locate crosswalks and pushbuttons, 
reducing confusion and errors at intersections.4 
They also cut down on delays when beginning 
to cross, allowing people to start during the 
WALK interval with far greater consistency and 
complete crossings before traffic begins to move.5

Broadly speaking, these interventions can 
increase the independence and confidence of 
pedestrians navigating public spaces.6 

While there are reports of these devices being 
used in the U.S. as early as the 1920s—mainly 
near schools for the blind—they were not 
included in the Federal Department of Transportation (DOT) Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD) 
until 2000.7

Currently in the U.S., APS systems are typically installed upon request along a specific route of travel for a 
particular individual or group. However, because of stagnant implementation of APS, increasingly class-action 
lawsuits are being brought in major cities on the claims that municipal transportation agencies are failing to 
make signaled intersections accessible for visually-impaired pedestrians, as required by the Americans with 
Disability Act (ADA). 

At a minimum, these lawsuits have generally resulted in agreements requiring jurisdictions to establish programs 
for pedestrians to formally request an APS. However, in some localities, these agreements have also required 
municipalities to install APS at existing intersections. For example, in Chicago in 2019, Disability Rights Advocates 
filed a class action lawsuit—later joined by the federal Department of Justice—that challenged Chicago’s 
pedestrian planning practices, which overlooked the safety needs of vision-impaired pedestrians.8 

At the time of the filing, less than 0.5 percent of Chicago’s more than 2,800 signalized intersections provided an 

4 • PEDESTRIAN HEAD START

APS.9 Following years of litigation, an agreement was reached in 2025 requiring that Chicago equip at least 71 
percent of its signalized intersections with pedestrian signals with APS in the next 10 years.10 The agreement 
also outlines a minimum number of intersections where APS must be installed each year (ranging from 70 to 245 
intersections per year).11

A similar lawsuit and agreement was reached in New York City in 2020, requiring that APS be installed at over 
10,000 intersections over the next 10 years, and that all signalized intersections be equipped with APS within 
15 years.12 

The Hawaiʻi Department of Transportation (HDOT)’s Pedestrian Master Plan and Toolbox discusses APS as an 
important feature to support disabled pedestrians. The plan states that APS should be installed where pedestrian 
push buttons are provided, in accordance with the Public Right-of-Way Accessibility Guidelines (PROWAG).13 

The guidelines serve as the technical standard for implementing ADA requirements and mandate that APS be 
installed at any new or modified pedestrian signal.14 Despite this, there is no publicly available information on 
how many intersections in Hawaiʻi have an APS, where intersections with an APS are located, or how pedestrians 
can request that a specific intersection be equipped with an APS. 

LEADING PEDESTRIAN INTERVALS (LPI)

LPIs enable the walk signal to turn on 3–7 seconds early for pedestrians, and in some cases also bicyclists, giving 
them a head-start before motor traffic gets the green.15 LPIs boost pedestrian visibility by providing pedestrians to 
establish their presence in the crosswalk and, in turn, significantly reduces accidents.16

LPIs specifically target one of the most common urban crash types: drivers failing to yield while turning at 
signalized intersections.17 For example, in Seattle about 35 percent of vehicle-pedestrian collisions  at signals 
involve turning vehicles failing to yield.18 

Without an LPI, pedestrians and parallel-turning vehicles receive the “go” signal at the same time, so a driver may 
quickly turn into an intersection and collide with a pedestrian who has just stepped off the curb. As such, LPIs 
are considered a low-cost proven countermeasure by the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) to improve 
pedestrian safety.19 

LPIs particularly help slower-walking pedestrians (such as children, seniors, and those using walkers, wheelchairs 
and other mobility assistance devices) by giving them a protected head start to cross the street. This is particularly 
useful at intersections with poor visibility.20 Transportation agencies often prioritize installing LPIs near schools, 
senior centers and transit hubs to support vulnerable populations.21

Because an LPI lets pedestrians move while parallel traffic is still red, blind or low-vision pedestrians, who use 
the sound of cars as a cue, may miss that early start and step out only after vehicles receive a green light, losing 
the safety benefit that LPIs provide.22 Therefore it is crucial to pair every LPI upgrade with an APS that meets ADA 
requirements. Additionally, transportation agencies often pair LPIs with “No Right Turn on Red” restrictions to 
better control for conflicts with vehicles turning right.23 

From a behavioral standpoint, LPIs generally do not rely on special user compliance beyond normal signal 
obedience. Pedestrians still cross during the walk signal and drivers still wait for green.24 Some public outreach may 
be helpful to educate road users about the new timing (e.g. signage indicating “Pedestrian Lead Interval in Effect” 
or similar).

LESSONS FROM 
NATIONAL PROGRAMS

Cities across the nation are implementing LPI and APS systems to improve the safety and accessibility of 
signalized intersections. The section highlights some cities—New York City, Seattle and Los Angeles—that have 
taken steps to implement LPIs city-wide, and the resulting pedestrian safety benefits of these efforts. The 
average cost for installing APS and LPI systems is also discussed in this section. 

BENEFITS

•  New York City: As part of its Vision Zero program, launched in 2014, New York City (NYC) deployed LPIs at 

thousands of intersections. A 2025 Columbia University study analyzing 2,869 LPI-treated intersections in NYC 
found a 33 percent reduction in total pedestrian injuries (fatal and non-fatal) at those sites.25 The effect was 
consistent across different types of intersections.26  

Importantly, fatal vehicle-pedestrian collisions in daytime hours dropped by 65 percent at treated intersections, 
indicating LPIs are especially effective at preventing the most severe collisions.27 NYC’s results underscore that a 
7-second head start can mean the difference between life and death for pedestrians.28

•  Seattle: Seattle began piloting LPIs in 2009 and has since expanded them aggressively as part of the city’s 

Vision Zero strategy.29 At intersections where LPIs were installed from 2009–2018, the city observed a 48 percent 
reduction in collisions involving vehicles failing to yield to pedestrians.30 Furthermore, serious injury and fatal 
pedestrian collisions at those locations declined by 34 percent.31  

As of early 2024, the Seattle Department of Transportation had implemented LPIs at 628 intersections, 

6 • PEDESTRIAN HEAD START

 
 
representing 64 percent of all of all feasible signalized intersections citywide.32 City officials have made LPIs 
a standard upgrade: Since 2019, Seattle’s policy is to evaluate adding an LPI whenever a signal is built or 
modernized, unless a specific site condition prevents it.33 This proactive, system-wide rollout regularly prevents 
vehicle-pedestrian collisions and has contributed to the success of the city’s Vision Zero program. 

• 

Los Angeles: The Los Angeles Department of Transportation (LADOT) began piloting LPIs in 2014, and has 
since rapidly expanded their implementation.34 Installation of LPIs is focused in downtown and other high-risk 
corridors identified in the city’s Vision Zero Action Plan.35 While LPIs had only been installed at 16 downtown 
intersections in 2016,36 by 2018 there were an estimated 154 intersections with LPIs.37 City leaders noted that 
“pedestrian head-start” signals are a proven way to encourage foot traffic in an urban environment while 
making crossing the street much safer.38  

Along the city’s high-injury network, LADOT continues to retrofit intersections with LPIs, along with other traffic 
calming measures such as reduced speed limits and curb extensions. In 2022, state legislation was enacted to 
require that all newly installed, state-owned traffic signals include the installation, activation and maintenance 
of LPIs, as well as APS.39 In certain areas—such as business, residential and school zones—this requirement also 
applies to state-owned intersections where maintenance work is conducted.40 Subsequently, Assembly Bill 1909 
was passed, allowing bicyclists to use LPIs in California.41

COSTS

One of the major advantages of LPIs is that they can be retrofitted into existing traffic signals relatively easily and 
inexpensively. Unlike physical infrastructure changes, such as curb extensions or a new traffic signal, an LPI is 
typically a software change to the signal controller timing. 

The FHWA estimates the cost to add an LPI at an intersection ranges from about $200 up to $1,200 per 
intersection.42 The lower end represents cases where only a simple controller setting change is needed, which 
can be done by municipal signal technicians.43 

In some cases, installing LPIs requires conducting a traffic study or installing a new APS device, which require 
additional fiscal resources. However, even the higher end of costs for LPIs is still very low compared to most road 
safety improvements. For example, a City and County of Honolulu official estimated that every mile of sidewalk 
costs approximately $1 million in newer communities with elevated existing curbs and gutters, and closer to $7 
to $10 million per mile in the island’s older communities.44 

The cost of inaction is even greater. Each year, over 100 people are killed in collisions on Hawaiʻi’s roads and 
highways.45 In 2025, there were 129 roadway fatalities in Hawaiʻi, a 20 percent increase from 2024 and the highest 
since 2007.46 Of those fatalities, 38 individuals (29 percent) were pedestrians.47 

Unfortunately this is reflective of the increasing safety threats faced by pedestrians. On Oʻahu the average annual 
number of pedestrian fatalities rose from 15.2 per year during the 2009–2013 time period, to 21 per year during 
the 2014–2018 time period (a 38 percent increase).48

Pedestrian crashes result in the most expensive roadway injuries. The FHWA estimated that every pedestrian 
fatality results in $10.5 million (2019 dollars) of medical care, work loss, household productivity, insurance 
administration and legal costs.49 Even less severe pedestrian injuries can result in an average of $627,462 in 
economic and social costs.50 As such, preventing even one pedestrian injury or fatality easily pays back the 
expense of deploying LPIs, even when installed at a high number of intersections.

RECOMMENDATIONS 
FOR HAWAI‘I

Pedestrian injuries and fatalities are on the rise in Hawaiʻi. This has had devastating impacts to the individuals 
directly involved in roadway crashes, and has resulted in immense social and economic costs for the greater 
public. There is an urgent need for pedestrian improvements that can be quickly deployed to increase roadway 
safety, and increase the walkability of our island communities. 

This report underscored the benefits of LPIs and APS devices, which are low-cost solutions that improve 
pedestrian safety and accessibility at signalized intersections. Recommendations to deploy these best practices 
in Hawaiʻi are provided below. 

1.  Establish a program to request and track LPIs and APS devices. 

The state should require HDOT, in coordination with county transportation agencies, to establish a formal, 
transparent and publicly accessible program to request, evaluate, implement and track LPIs and APS devices at 
signalized intersections. This program would serve as the foundation for a systematic approach to pedestrian 
safety and accessibility, replacing the current ad-hoc and opaque practices that leave many intersections unsafe 
and inaccessible—particularly for blind, low-vision, elderly and mobility-impaired pedestrians.

At present, there is no publicly available information on how many signalized intersections in Hawaiʻi are 
equipped with APS or LPIs, where those intersections are located, or how pedestrians can request these safety 
features. This lack of transparency and formal process not only impedes equitable access to public rights-of-way, 
but also exposes the state and counties to potential legal risk under the ADA. In other jurisdictions, the absence 
of a clear APS request and implementation program has been a central factor in costly class-action litigation, 
resulting in court-mandated timelines and large-scale retrofits. Proactively establishing a program now would 
allow Hawaiʻi to act strategically and cost-effectively, rather than under court order.

These practices have been employed by some of the agencies highlighted in this report, such as the Seattle 
Department of Transportation, who provides an online map of LPI locations, and statistics on the number of LPIs 
implemented every year. 

2.  Require LPIs and APS at All New and Modified Signalized Intersections

HDOT, as well as the counties, should be required to install LPI and APS devices at all newly-constructed 
signalized intersections, as well as at existing intersections undergoing signal installation, major reconstruction, 
or substantial maintenance work. This policy mirrors California’s Assembly Bill No. 2264, which treats proven 
pedestrian safety treatments as a standard design requirement rather than an optional enhancement.

Embedding LPIs and APS into routine capital and maintenance projects ensures that pedestrian safety 
improvements are implemented systematically and cost-effectively, avoiding the higher costs and delays 

8 • PEDESTRIAN HEAD START

 
associated with retrofitting intersections after construction. Making these treatments standard practice also 
promotes consistency across jurisdictions and ensures compliance with ADA and PROWAG. 

By institutionalizing LPIs and APS as default features of new and modified intersections, the state can steadily 
expand its pedestrian safety network while ensuring that blind, low-vision, and other vulnerable pedestrians 
benefit equally from signalized crossings.

3.  Require a certain number of LPIs be implemented each year, in tandem with APS devices and other 

safety features. 

In addition to requiring LPIs and APS in new and modified intersections, HDOT and the counties should be 
required to implement a minimum number of LPIs each year at existing signalized intersections, paired with 
APS devices and other complementary pedestrian safety features where appropriate. Establishing an annual 
implementation requirement shifts pedestrian safety from a discretionary activity to a measurable, accountable 
public safety obligation—one that reflects the urgency of rising pedestrian fatalities across the state.

This recommendation calls for HDOT, in coordination with county transportation agencies, to install LPIs 
at a minimum of 20 to 30 signalized intersections per year statewide. Per the guidance of the FHWA,51 when 
considering which intersections should be prioritized for LPI improvements, municipalities should consider the 
following factors: 

•  Crash History: Intersections with multiple crashes or a history of severe injury/fatal pedestrian crashes 

should be considered a priority.

•  Vulnerable Populations: LPIs should also be prioritized where school-aged children, the elderly, disabled, or 

other vulnerable populations are expected to cross.

•  Pedestrian Crossing Volumes: LPIs are useful at intersections with high pedestrian volumes. The estimated 

exposure (product of pedestrian and turning traffic volumes) may be another consideration.

•  One-Way Streets or at T-intersections: Where left-turning vehicles aren’t typically expected to yield to 

oncoming vehicles, LPIs may be useful to increase yielding to pedestrians. 

• 

Intersection Visibility: LPIs may be prioritized where crosswalk visibility is limited or restricted. This could 
include where stopped vehicles, vegetation, or poor lighting decreases visibility. 

Crucially, every new LPI installation should be paired with APS that meets ADA and PROWAG standards. Federal 
guidance and professional consensus make clear that LPIs without APS can unintentionally disadvantage blind 
and low-vision pedestrians, who may miss the early walk interval and lose the intended safety benefit. Pairing 
these treatments ensures equity, legal compliance, and maximum effectiveness. 

Where appropriate, LPIs should also be coordinated with additional safety measures such as “No Turn on 
Red” restrictions, improved crosswalk markings, lighting upgrades, or signal timing adjustments that reflect 
pedestrian walking speeds.

To support effective implementation, HDOT should be required to publish an annual LPI implementation plan 
identifying candidate intersections, selection criteria, and anticipated timelines. Progress toward the annual 
target should be reported publicly, along with crash and safety performance indicators where available. This 
transparency allows lawmakers and the public to evaluate whether investments are being directed to the 
highest-need locations and whether the program is achieving its intended outcomes.
