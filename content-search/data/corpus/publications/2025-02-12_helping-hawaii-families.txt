Hawaiʻi Appleseed is committed to a more socially and economically just Hawaiʻi, where everyone has genuine 
opportunities to achieve economic security and fulfill their potential. We change systems to address inequity 
and foster greater opportunity by conducting data analysis and research to address income inequality, educating 
policymakers and the public, engaging in collaborative problem solving and coalition building, and advocating 
for policy and systems change.

The work of Hawaiʻi Appleseed is about people. The issues we work on—housing, food, wages, mobility, the state 
budget and taxation, and racial and indigenous equity—are important because they ensure people have access 
to shelter, sustenance, and the means to survive and thrive individually and collectively. Addressing these issues 
requires the knowledge and expertise of the people that have first-hand experience and live with the adverse 
consequences of our flawed systems.

2 • HELPING HAWAIʻI’S FAMILIES

INTRODUCTION

ALL OF HAWAIʻI’s families deserve the opportunity 
to thrive without worrying whether they’ll be able 
to make ends meet each month. Yet Hawaiʻi’s 
unaffordable cost of living continues to act as a 
barrier to long-term economic security. This crisis has 
driven many low- to middle-income families to other 
states in search of lower costs and higher-paying jobs. 
Many of the working families who stay are at risk of 
falling into poverty, debt, and even houselessness. 

Tax credits are an effective way to give these families 
the money they need to pay for their everyday 
expenses, such as food, rent, and medical care. 
Improving existing tax credits, as well as implementing 
a new state-level child tax credit, would help lift up 
tens of thousands of the local families that power our 
economy, strengthening our shared prosperity.

The need for robust tax credits continues to grow along 
with the increasingly unaffordable cost of living in 
Hawaiʻi. In 2022, 15,664 local residents left Hawaiʻi for 
other states in the U.S.1 Much of this outmigration has 
been driven by how difficult it is for families to make 
ends meet, even if they work full-time.

• 

• 

In 2023, the median income for a single person was 
$60,346 per year.2 For comparison, the median rent 
was $1,940 a month, or $23,280 for the year.3 

In 2022, 10.2 percent of Hawaiʻi’s residents were 
living in poverty. Overall, 44 percent of households 
in Hawaiʻi were either living in poverty, or were 
under the Asset-Constrained, Income-Limited, 

Employed (ALICE) threshold.4 ALICE households are 
not technically living in poverty, but they are still 
struggling to make ends meet. 

A family of four with two young children would have to 
earn over $107,000 to stay above the ALICE threshold 
and achieve basic economic security.5 This amount of 
money is simply out of reach for most working parents, 
given how few available jobs pay a living wage. In 
2025, a living wage for that same Hawaiʻi family of 
four—when both adults are working—is $36.43 an hour 
in Honolulu,6 compared to the Hawaiʻi state minimum 
wage of just $14 an hour. 

Food service jobs make up 12.4 percent of all jobs 
in Hawaiʻi, followed by retail and sales jobs at 8.6 
percent.7 These jobs often pay low wages, and do 
not provide opportunities for non-college educated 
workers to adequately support their families.

Underscoring this point, families with children were 
significantly more likely to be in poverty compared to 
the rest of the population. Further, nearly one out of 
four single mothers in Hawaiʻi live beneath the poverty 
line (Figure 1). 

Without help from the government, many people 
living in poverty are unable to reach lasting economic 
security. This partly explains why poverty carries over 
from one generation to the next, as children who grow 
up in low-income households are less likely to attend 
college and find well paying jobs later in life.8

Tax Credits Make Living in Hawaiʻi More Affordable

Tax credits are extra money that qualifying people receive when filing their tax returns. They are intended to 
lower the income taxes paid by certain populations, particularly low- to middle-income people. There are two 
major advantages of using tax credits to help people over other types of assistance.

1.  Tax credits are simpler to apply for than government programs. 

If someone meets the basic requirements, they can automatically claim the credit when they file their 
tax return. In comparison, it can take more time and effort to apply for government programs like the 
Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP). SNAP recipients have to undergo a lengthy application 
process, which includes an interview, providing documentation, and getting recertified every 6 months. 

2.  Tax credits can be used for any expenses.  

Tax credits can be spent on food, housing, transportation, or anything else that people need in their day-to-
day life. This gives recipients the freedom to decide how they will spend their money, without restrictions. 
On the other hand, SNAP recipients can only spend their benefits on food, excluding hot meals. Likewise, 
housing assistance from the federal government can only be used to pay rent.

HAWAIʻI’S TAX CREDITS

Although Hawaiʻi has many different tax credits, there is a special category of “social welfare” credits that focus 
on helping low- to middle-income people. Within this category, the four most widely-used tax credits are:

•  The Refundable Food/Excise Tax Credit 

This tax credit was created to offset the impact of the general excise tax (GET), Hawaiʻi’s tax on goods 
and services. Although the GET raises the price of basic necessities for everyone, it affects low-income 
households the most. These households spend a larger portion of their income on basic necessities like 
groceries, which higher-income households can better afford. This Refundable Food/Excise Tax Credit is 
available to individuals earning less than $40,000 and couples earning less than $60,000.10 

•  Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) 

The EITC is intended to benefit low- to middle-income people who are employed, especially if they have 
children. There is a federal EITC that has been in place since 1975, and Hawaiʻi has its own state-level EITC 
that is worth 40 percent of the federal credit. The income limit and credit amount both depend on the 
number of people in a household. In the case of a single parent with three children, the EITC allows for a 
maximum income of $59,899 in tax year 2024.11 The state EITC was also made refundable in 2022, which 
means that low-income households that do not owe much in taxes can still benefit from the credit. 

•  Household and Dependent Care Services Tax Credit (HDCSTC) 

This credit is also known as the Child and Dependent Care Services Tax Credit (CDCTC). It reimburses 
taxpayers for 15 to 25 percent of the cost of providing care to qualifying dependents in their household. 
These “qualifying dependents” are defined as children under the age of 13 and individuals who cannot care 
for themselves. There is no income limit, but the credit is capped at $2,500 for one dependent and $5,000 for 
two or more dependents.12 

•  Tax Credit for Low-Income Household Renters 

This tax credit is narrowly targeted at very low-income households that typically do not have enough money 
to pay their rent out-of-pocket. The income limit for this credit is $30,000, and the taxpayer must have 
also paid at least $1,000 in rent for that year to qualify. The credit itself is only worth $50 for each personal 
exemption that the taxpayer can claim for their state income taxes.13 In 2022, this amounted to about $97 on 
average for each taxpayer. Since this credit has not been adjusted for inflation, the number of taxpayers who 
claim it has declined significantly over time. 

The data in the following figures is for the 2022 tax year. Since then, the income limits for the Refundable Food/
Excise Tax Credit have been increased and the tax credit amounts were doubled. In addition, the Household and 
Dependent Care Services Tax Credit has been expanded—though this credit needs to be fixed through additional 
legislation before the changes can actually come into effect.

Almost a third of taxpayers claimed the Refundable Food/Excise Tax Credit, and more than one out of 10 
taxpayers claimed the state EITC (Figure 2). These tax credits may be small when compared to the budgets of 
most households, but they can mean the difference between a family paying its bills for the month or not. On 
average, the Household and Dependent Care Services Tax Credit provides the largest credit amounts (Figure 3). 

However, it is also important to look at how the credits are spread across different income groups. Out of these 
credits, the Refundable Food/Excise Tax Credit and the EITC funnel the largest share of their benefits to low- and 
middle-income people. At the other end, this data shows that the Household and Dependent Care Services Tax 
Credit tends to benefit higher-income households more, since they have more money to afford the upfront cost 
of child and dependent care (Figure 4).

THE CHILD TAX CREDIT

First established in 1997, the federal Child Tax Credit (CTC) provides financial relief to families with children—
particularly those with lower incomes. The federal CTC offers a $2,000 credit per child under 18.17 

A temporary increase under the American Rescue Plan (ARP) in 2021 boosted that amount to $3,600 for children 
aged 0–6, and $3,000 for those aged 6–17. In Hawaiʻi, 162,000 families with 272,000 children received the federal 
CTC in 2021.

This expansion significantly reduced child poverty, keeping 2.9 million children out of poverty nationwide, 
especially those from communities of color. However, after the expansion ended, the U.S. poverty rate doubled 
from 5.2 percent to 12.4 percent.18

Why Hawaiʻi Needs Its Own Child Tax Credit

1.  The state does not provide enough tax relief for working families. 

Although Hawaiʻi offers a number of tax credits that assist low-income residents, these credits are too small 
to permanently lift people out of poverty. For example, the Refundable Food/Excise Tax Credit is a great tool 
for helping families offset the regressive GET, but it only gives out an average of $118 to each taxpayer. A 
larger amount of money is needed to get families on their feet in the long term, guaranteeing that they do not 
fall back into financial insecurity. 

2.  Recent tax relief has not targeted those who need it the most. 

House Bill 2404 (2024), which was signed into law by Governor Josh Green in June of 2024, adjusted Hawaiʻi’s 
income tax brackets and standard deduction upward. It will cut income taxes for most people, but high-
income people will ultimately receive the largest benefit. In 2025, the top 1 percent will receive a tax cut of 
more than $6,000, compared to $335 for the bottom 20 percent.19 These changes will also lower Hawaiʻi's tax 
revenue by $1.2 to $1.4 billion by 2031, taking away funds that will be needed to invest in state programs and 
services for Hawaiʻi’s most vulnerable households.

TAX CREDITS

There are two good options for the Hawaiʻi State Legislature to invest more money into tax credits to help 
Hawaiʻi's families.

1. Create a State-Level Child Tax Credit

The first option is to create a state-level child tax credit for Hawaiʻi. Proposed legislation in 2025 calls for a 
credit worth up to $650 per child. This amount would phased-out above incomes of $40,000, with an income 
limit of $115,000 per household. This version of the CTC would cost around $83 million each year.20 An income 
limit of $100,000 has also been suggested and could drive a larger portion of the benefits to families in the 
ALICE population.

2. Expand the State Earned Income Tax Credit

The second option would be to boost the existing state EITC for households that have children. In 2022, the value 
of the state EITC was raised from 20 percent to 40 percent of the federal EITC. Proposed legislation in 2025 would 
increase that percentage to 50 percent if the household has at least one child under the age of 18. Figure 6 shows 
how this boost could affect local families in different income groups.

On top of the state EITC they already receive, people who qualify for this expanded EITC would see an additional 
tax cut of $512 on average. In a similar way to the CTC, the expanded EITC would primarily benefit Hawaiʻi’s low- 
to middle-income population. This proposal would come at a cost of just $30 million.

FUNDING OUR FUTURE

These tax credits can be paid for by raising taxes on a small number of wealthy people. There are several ways to 
achieve this, but two that are very good at targeting tax increases only to those who can easily afford to pay are:

1. Closing the Capital Gains Loophole

Hawaiʻi can better support low-income working families by changing how it taxes income from investments, 
called “capital gains.” Currently, the state taxes capital gains at a lower rate (7.25 percent) than regular income 
(up to 11 percent for the highest earners). This loophole greatly benefits wealthy households that make up a 
large part of their income through investments. In 2025, someone who earns $325,000 from a regular job would 
pay $25,966 in state income taxes. If they earned all of this money through capital gains instead, this person 
would pay a smaller tax of $23,563.

Taxing capital gains at the same rate as regular income could bring in as much as $79 million each year.23 This 
extra money could cover most—if not all—of the funds needed for a state CTC or EITC expansion.

2. Increasing Conveyance Tax Rates on Mansions

Hawaiʻi’s conveyance tax is a fee paid when real estate is sold or transferred. The amount of tax depends on the 
property’s sale price, and whether it will be used as a primary residence. More expensive properties and non-
owner-occupied transactions come with higher tax rates. Some of this revenue flows to the state’s general fund, 
while the remaining portions are set aside for land conservation and affordable housing. 

In 2025, a legislative proposal would convert the conveyance tax structure to a marginal tax system, while raising 
the rates on high-value properties. This would avoid the “cliff effect,” where properties that cross over into the 
next value bracket are taxed at a much higher rate. It could also boost the revenue from the conveyance tax by a 
third or more.24
