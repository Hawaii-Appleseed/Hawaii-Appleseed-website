Dear Chair Yamashita, Vice Chair Takenouchi, and Members of the Committee,

Thank you for the opportunity to testify on behalf of Hawaiʻi Appleseed in strong support of HB 1662. This bill would implement a state-level Child Tax Credit (CTC) for Hawaiʻi, one that builds on the incredible success of the CTC at the national level. The CTC works by giving a tax refund to families with children, which they can use to pay for food, housing, education, and other essential expenses.

Background
The COVID-19 pandemic had a disastrous impact on the livelihoods of Hawaiʻi's working families. The unemployment rate skyrocketed to over 20%, and the accompanying economic recession has had a lasting impact on poverty in Hawaiʻi.

In 2022, 29% of Hawaiʻi's population belonged to the Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed (ALICE) category, with an additional 15% residing below the poverty line. In total, this means that 44% of Hawaiʻi's residents had some difficulty with making ends meet.[1] Furthermore, many ALICE families are on the brink of poverty, leaving them vulnerable to the unexpected costs that come with events such as medical emergencies or car repairs. This burden is only magnified for parents who must care for their children’s needs on top of their other household expenses.

  

The Child Tax Credit
The federal CTC has provided critical support to working families in the US since 1997. It was expanded under the provisions of the American Rescue Plan in 2021, which increased the maximum CTC amount to $3,600 for each child aged 0–5 and $3,000 for each child aged 6–17. This historic investment lifted 2.9 million children in the US out of poverty—nearly cutting the child poverty rate in half and making the CTC the single most effective anti-poverty program in the history of the United States.

  

A total of 162,000 families in Hawaiʻi (with more than 272,000 children) reaped the benefits of the expanded federal CTC, receiving an average tax refund of $2,426.[2] Analysis shows that had this expansion been made permanent, the child poverty rate in Hawaiʻi would have dropped from 9.8 percent to just 5 percent.[3] 

However, when the federal CTC expansion ended in 2021, there was a significant rollback in its impact on poverty. The national poverty rate more than doubled from 5.2% to 12.4% by 2022. At the same time, there was a 25% surge in food insecurity among households with children in the U.S. between January and July of 2022. To compensate for this, 14 states have created their own CTCs.

In prior versions of HB 1662 (before it was amended by the House Committee on Finance), the state CTC would have given a tax credit of $650 per child under the age of 18. Above an income threshold of $40,000, the credit would then be gradually phased out by 10% for each additional increment of $7,500, with an income cap of $115,000. Furthermore, this CTC would have included refundability, ensuring that even individuals with minimal income tax obligations could receive the entire amount of the credit. The House Committee on Finance amended the bill to remove the original credit amounts altogether, which means that HB 1662 in its current form does not have any credit amounts on paper.

We urge the members of this committee to restore the CTC’s credit amounts that were proposed in the first draft of HB 1662. Furthermore, we encourage this committee to increase the state CTC beyond these original amounts, maximizing the benefits that would be distributed to our working families.

Although the original version of HB 1662 came with an estimated cost of around $84 million, it would have benefited 176,000 children in Hawaiʻi, targeted towards the families that need them the most. The recipients of this credit primarily would have been children belonging to families in poverty and those under the broader ALICE category. Consequently, the CTC would lift up the majority of parents who are financially insecure, even if they are not technically beneath the poverty line.
