Testimony of the Hawaiʻi Appleseed Center for Law and Economic Justice
Support for H.B. 1540 – Relating to School Meals
House Committee on Education
Thursday February 8, 2024, at 2:00PM
                                                                                                                

Dear Chair Woodson, Vice Chair La Chica, and members of the Committee:

Thank you for the opportunity to submit testimony in support of H.B. 1540, which would require eligible schools to participate in the federal Community Eligibility Provision (CEP), which provides federal funds to help schools offer free meals to all students, and appropriates money for any difference between the federal funds amount and cost of providing free school meals. 

School breakfast and lunch provide our students with the essential nutrition they need in order to continue to learn throughout the school day. Access to this essential school supply should not be withheld based on students’ ability to pay.  

Federal eligibility for free or reduced price meals requires that households earn below 130% or 185% of the federal poverty level ($44,850 or $63,825, respectively, annually for a family of four in 2024). However, Hawaiʻi’s costs of living require that a family with two adults and two children make at least $104,000 to simply survive.[1] This leaves many households ineligible for any meaningful assistance yet unable to make ends meet. 

Requiring participation in CEP is a tested strategy that many states have used on their journey to achieving a universal free school meals program. Leveraging CEP potentially allows participating schools to draw down more federal funds than they would otherwise based on the number of identified students living in poverty (ISP) that attend that school. In school year 2022-2023, 38% (106) of DOE’s 257 sites participated in CEP.[2] In October 2023, the USDA issued a rule change lowering the CEP eligibility percentage to 25% resulting in a more than 30% increase in the number of DOE schools now eligible for CEP (72 to 162 eligible schools).[3] 

It should be noted that some schools face significant administrative and financial barriers to participate in CEP and requiring participation may negatively impact those schools meal programs. For example, a school with an identified student percentage (students directly certified for free or reduced price meals through mean tested programs like SNAP) of 30% but has a student body where 50% of children are certified through free and reduced price meal applications, that school might lose out additional federal funding by opting into the CEP with only a 30% ISP. Thus, we want to emphasize the need for technical assistance and support to be provided to schools to successfully enroll and stay enrolled in the CEP program and highlight any additional monies or staff that may be required to provide that much needed support.

Thank you for the opportunity to provide testimony on this important matter. We are pleased that the Hawaiʻi legislature is paying attention to this important issue because hungry keiki cannot learn. Thank you for your consideration of this bill.
