Testimony of the Hawaiʻi Appleseed Center for Law and Economic Justice
Support for HB 1779 – Relating to School Meals
House Committee on Finance
Wednesday, March 4, 2026 at 10:00AM
                                                                                                                




Dear Chair Todd, Vice Chair Takenouchi, and members of the Committee:


Thank you for the opportunity to submit testimony in strong support of HB 1779, which would expand free school meal coverage to all Department of Education (HIDOE) public and public charter school students regardless of whether the student is eligible beginning with the 2029-2030 school year.
Providing free school meals to all students does more than just address food insecurity. Studies of universal free school meals in other states have shown significant improvements in test scores and academic performance, and research consistently links proper nutrition to better healthy development and learning outcomes.[1] UFSM also creates a more inclusive and supportive school environment, and can even support Farm-to-School initiatives and local farmers by incorporating fresh, locally grown produce into school meals. 
We were very pleased with the legislature’s passage of SB 1300 (now Act 139) last session, and Governor Josh Green’s stated commitment to achieving free school meals for all students in the next five years.[2] This bill puts that commitment to paper, without any financial impact to the state until Fiscal Year 2030. 
Using meal count data from HIDOE, we estimate the cost of the bill would be ~$16.3 million to cover HIDOE students at current participation rates. However, this doesn't account for other ongoing costs the Department will incur regardless of this proposal, which explains the discrepancy between our estimates. The Department is also short an additional $36 million, bringing the total program cost (if this bill passes) to ~$52 million in FY2030.
We also estimate an additional $1.3 million to extend this benefit to public charter school students. This does not account for administrative costs. See our cost analysis attached to this testimony. 
If the price tag for this service is cause for concern, there are other ways the state might consider making up the shortfall that don’t place the burden on public school parents. Several bills being considered by this committee this week–and hopefully later on this session–would appropriately tax corporations and Hawaiʻi’s wealthiest residents, raising anywhere from $36 million to $260 million – enough to fund this service multiple times over. 
We are pleased that the Hawaiʻi legislature is paying attention to this important issue. Thank you for your consideration of this bill.
