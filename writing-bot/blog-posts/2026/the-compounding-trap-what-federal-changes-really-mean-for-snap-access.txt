The compounding trap: What federal changes really mean for SNAP access

Recent changes to federal SNAP policy are built on a cruel contradiction: they demand that recipients jump through increasingly impossible hoops to keep benefits, while stripping away the very resources states need to process them. The result is a vicious cycle in which federal cuts drive up administrative strain, errors trigger massive state penalties, and everyday families bear the cost.
Even as federal SNAP spending has decreased by
$11 billion
between last year and now, rising costs of gas, groceries and rent make it harder and harder for families to make ends meet. And as states are now forced to shoulder the cost of a program that so many residents depend on, federal spending on the
deeply unpopular
Iran war has now reached
$47.1 billion
.
For Hawaiʻi—already wrestling with a 40-year-old eligibility system and an understaffed Department of Human Services (DHS) office—the detrimental cuts from H.R. 1 hit hard.

Work Requirements Now Harder To Meet
Nowhere is this compounding pressure more visible than in the sudden expansion of federal work requirements—shifting the burden directly onto everyday families. Under H.R. 1, the following groups now face new or expanded requirements:
Adults ages 55–64
;
Parents and caregivers whose youngest child is 14 or older;
Veterans;
Individuals experiencing homelessness; and
Former foster youth ages 18–24.
Those who fall in these categories must now document 20 hours per week of work, volunteering, job training or education to keep their benefits. Those who can’t meet the requirement will have their SNAP benefits limited to 3 months during a 3-year period. After that, their benefits stop. DHS has
estimated
that about 16,000 SNAP recipients ages 55–64, and about 10,000 households with a child 14 or older, fall under the new rules.
Research
consistently shows
that work requirements do not increase employment. In practice, these types of requirements are just one more burden added to the plates of everyday families and Hawaiʻi’s SNAP office alike; SNAP recipients are forced to clear new hurdles just to continue to afford groceries, and the state is left responsible for a new administrative challenge.

The Vicious Cycle of Federal Cuts and State Penalties
These new federal policies demand more from already overburdened state agencies–all while stripping away the very resources needed to run them. In this cycle, underfunded systems are inevitably set up to fail, and then penalized for their failure, leading to additional strain.
For example, before October 1st, the federal government covered 50 percent of SNAP’s administrative costs. Now it covers only 25 percent, leaving the state to pay the rest. That would mean as much as
$19 million
in additional annual administrative costs from this shift. As of last year, the SNAP office had between a
25–33 percent vacancy rate
for positions crucial to processing phone calls and applications. Anticipating the added burden, DHS has already made a
request
for $13.4 million to cover 101 permanent positions for the current state fiscal year.
The loss of federal administrative funds also means that SNAP outreach providers—typically food banks, pantries, and their partners—stand to lose critical funding for outreach and application assistance. These providers will have to cover the gap with philanthropic funds or by cutting essential services, a shift that is already underway on some of the islands.
All of this ultimately puts strain on the system, driving up the payment error rate–the determining factor in how much the state will have to pay in benefits the following year. For Hawaiʻi, that could mean as much as
$139 million toward SNAP benefits
when the state cost share begins next year. This is the compounding trap: the federal cuts reduce what’s available to run the program well, leading to more potential errors, and lowering accessibility for recipients and applicants.
Where We Go From Here
All that is left now is to determine how the state chooses to respond, and how the community shows up for SNAP. The department pursued a waiver for a delay in these work requirements, and spent the past year flagging those who might be newly subject. The current priority for DHS is to keep the payment error rate down in anticipation of next year’s cost share—but they have to pay for the administrative share
now
.
As the 2027 legislative session gears up in January, there is an opportunity to address the damage that is being done, and the utmost priority should be to keep SNAP alive.
Here is what that could look like for Hawaiʻi:
Supporting the current budget request for the SNAP office itself.
With the funding to cover the administrative cost share, this may be the most direct way to protect benefits now and keep the SNAP office afloat as additional penalties loom ahead.
Rolling out the new Benefits Eligibility System.
Set to pilot this fall and roll out statewide in early 2027,
this new system
could help to bring errors down over time, and would allow for the department to implement SNAP statewide policies that were
passed last session
.
Supplemental state outreach funding for community partners.
Thousands of eligible families still aren’t enrolled in SNAP
. Additional funding could help outreach providers to guide people through work requirements, allowing them to focus on populations that need more support like those in rural communities, or those with language access needs.
Sold as a fix for “waste, fraud and abuse,” the Trump Administration frames these federal policies as the cleanup of a broken system. In practice, shifting administrative burdens and costs onto states creates a compounding trap that threatens to end SNAP as we know it.
Hawaiʻi’s choice is no longer just about managing a budget shortfall—it is about whether the state will step in to prevent these federal changes from becoming a death blow to food security for the roughly 160,000 state residents who rely on SNAP.
