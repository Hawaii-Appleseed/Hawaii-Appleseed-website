While further research is needed to understand the full scope of how public transportation funding is allocated 
in Hawaiʻi, it is clear from our report that a major share of Hawaiʻi’s federal and state transportation budgets are 
dedicated to accommodating and encouraging vehicle travel. This is particularly true of HDOT, which acts as the 
central hub to plan and spend an enormous amount of government funding for the state’s transportation system.

Given the HDOT’s prioritization of roadway expansion over alternative forms of transportation, it is not 
surprising that vehicle travel is rising in Hawaiʻi. According to the Hawaiʻi Climate Change Commission, total 
vehicle travel in Hawaiʻi has increased by almost 40 percent over the last 25 years and is anticipated to grow 
without intervention.3 Vehicle travel negatively impacts individuals and communities in a variety of ways. 

Unmet Mobility Needs 

HDOT’s prioritization of vehicle travel has manifested itself in a transportation system that is largely 
inaccessible for individuals who do not drive. According to 2023 estimates, while the statewide highway system 
includes approximately 9,800 miles of vehicle lanes, there are only 168 miles of state-owned sidewalks, and 177 
miles of state-owned bikeways.4

While a large majority (66.5 percent) of individuals in Hawaiʻi report driving alone to work,5 commuting data is 
insufficient in capturing the diverse reasons that individuals travel, such as school, recreation and healthcare. 
Limited mobility can reduce access to these destinations, which can have downstream impacts to social and 
economic wellbeing. 

Those who cannot, or choose not to drive are the most likely to have limited mobility options. Based on 
national data from an automotive marketing research firm, roughly 30 percent of the population does not 
drive.6 This segment of the population is likely to include, but not limited to: 

Young People: Roughly 20 percent of Hawaiʻi residents are under the age of 18.7 While young 
people often have immense travel needs, individuals in Hawaiʻi (as in most U.S. states) 
cannot legally drive until age 16. Based on 2021 data, even for those of legal driving age, only 
40 percent of individuals aged 19 and under are licensed.8

Older Adults: Over 21 percent of Hawaiʻi residents are 65 years old or older.9 According to data 
from the U.S. Census and the FHWA, licensing rates begin to decline for middle-aged drivers at 
age 55 and only 69 percent of adults 85 and older in the U.S. have a driver’s license.10 

Disabled Populations: About 7 percent of Hawaiʻi residents under the age of 65 have some 
form of a disability.11 Disabilities can restrict or eliminate the possibility of operating a 
vehicle. Disabled populations are also disproportionately burdened by unsafe pedestrian 
infrastructure due to challenges in navigating physical spaces.

Low Income: Approximately 10 percent of Hawaiʻi residents fall below the official federal 
poverty line.12 Low income residents are less likely to commute by driving and more likely to 
depend on walking, biking, and transit. According to national data, an estimated 30 percent 
of bus riders have a household income less than $15,000 and 55 percent of all transit riders 
have a household income less than $50,000.13

Traffic Congestion

Increases in vehicle travel leads to more traffic congestion, as more vehicles on the road result in slower speeds, 
longer travel times, and increased delays. Traffic congestion is particularly challenging in Honolulu, where 
according to a 2019 study, drivers spend an estimated 64 hours in traffic each year.14 In addition to negative 
mental and physical health impacts, traffic congestion costs the state an estimated $693.1 million annually 
through lost time and productivity from hours spent in traffic.15 

Contrary to popular belief, increasing roadway capacity through the construction of new roads or lanes has 
been found to increase congestion, not decrease it. This is because when roadways are congested, drivers often 
avoid driving, or use other modes of transportation. 

While new or expanded roadways can help mitigate congestion initially, they eventually end up attracting new 
drivers who would have formerly avoided them within two to five years (see Figure 1). This can generally be 
described as the “build it and they will come” concept. This has been validated by countless studies, including 
research by scholars Turner and Duranton, who found an almost perfect correlation between lane mile 
construction and vehicle travel in all metropolitan areas in the U.S. between 1980 and 2000.16

Financial Costs to the Public and Private Individual

As vehicle travel increases, the cost of operating and maintaining roadway infrastructure, as well as the costs 
of vehicle ownership, also increase. The state already spends a staggering amount to accommodate vehicle 
travel. According to the 2021 “Costs of the Vehicle Economy in Hawaiʻi” report, the total annual cost of Hawaiʻi’s 
“vehicle economy” is $21.8 billion.18

Roadway Safety 

Each year, over 100 people are killed in 
collisions on Hawaiʻi’s roads and highways.21 
Higher vehicle travel typically leads to 
greater exposure and more frequent conflicts 
between vehicles for all roadways users. This 
also increases the risk of collisions involving 
pedestrians, bicyclists, and other vulnerable 
roadway users. 

Although pedestrians and bicyclists make up a very small percentage of roadway users, they comprise a 
disproportionately large share of traffic fatalities. For example, from 2010 to 2022, pedestrian deaths on average 
comprised 25.8 percent of the state’s total traffic fatalities.22 During that time period, pedestrian deaths reached 
a high in 2018, when 44 pedestrians were killed, representing nearly 38 percent of the total roadway fatalities.23

Climate Change and Energy

As vehicle travel increases, more fuel is consumed, leading to higher greenhouse gas emissions that contribute 
to climate change. Reliance on fossil fuels also raises energy demand. This is particularly important when 
considering the state’s goal to be carbon negative by 2045. While the number of electric cars is increasing, 
emissions from the ground transportation sector in Hawaiʻi remain high and are projected to continue to grow. 
According to the Hawaiʻi Climate Commission, to achieve Hawaiʻi’s negative carbon emissions goal by 2045, 
vehicle travel cannot continue to grow.24

NAVAHINE SETTLEMENT TO REDUCE VEHICLE TRAVEL 

Navahine v. Hawaiʻi Department of Transportation was the world’s first youth-led constitutional 
climate case seeking to address climate pollution from the transportation sector.25 The case 
was first brought forward in June 2022 by 13 youth from across Hawaiʻi, who sued HDOT and its 
director, as well as the state and governor, arguing that the transportation sector’s high levels of 
climate pollution contribute to dangerous climate change in violation of rights protected under 
Hawaiʻi’s state constitution.26 

The lawsuit aimed to hold HDOT accountable in achieving the state’s mandated goal to become 
carbon negative by 2045.27 Parties to the lawsuit came to an agreement in June, 2024. The 
settlement requires HDOT to:

• 

Take steps to establish a GHG emissions reduction plan (GHG Reduction Plan); 

•  Create a unit and positions within HDOT to take the lead on coordinating the mission of 

GHG reduction;

• 

Establish a youth council to advise on HDOT’s commitments;

•  Make investments in clean transportation infrastructure, including completing the 

pedestrian, bicycle, and transit network in 5 years, and dedicating a minimum of $40 million 
to expanding the electric vehicle charging network by 2030.28 (HDOT is currently in the 
process of defining the extent of this network); and

•  Develop interim targets for reducing vehicle travel, and a new methodology for assessing 

each infrastructure project’s GHG emissions and vehicle travel impacts.

As discussed in this report and highlighted in the Navahine settlement, reducing vehicle travel is 
a critical step in decarbonizing the transportation sector, and thus also mandated by the state’s 
clean energy goals. 

While vehicle electrification can often become the main focus of decarbonization efforts, electric 
vehicles still contribute to many of the issues caused by rising vehicle travel, including the high 
public costs of constructing and maintaining vehicle infrastructure, increasing roadway safety 
concerns, and others. Moreover, because of their high costs, electric vehicles are financially 
inaccessible for many Hawaiʻi residents.

TRANSPORTATION 
FUNDING IN HAWAI‘I

Transportation infrastructure, programs and services are funded through a combination of federal, state and 
county sources. This report analyzes federal and state transportation budgets in Hawaiʻi, with a focus on what 
type of transportation infrastructure is being funded and what type of roadway users are being served.

The greatest revenue source for transportation infrastructure in Hawaiʻi is federal funding, which is allocated 
to HDOT, the counties, and the Honolulu Authority for Rapid Transportation (HART). 

As demonstrated in this report, HDOT allocates a majority of its funding (federal and state) to vehicle 
infrastructure. In comparison, the counties allocated a greater amount of their federal funding to other 
transportation modes, primarily public transportation. Notably, HDOT has a significantly larger budget than the 
counties, as the state is the primary recipient of federal transportation funding.

The Statewide Transportation Improvement Program (STIP) is a federally-mandated planning document 
that outlines transportation projects and initiatives funded with federal, state and local resources over a 
multi-year period. It serves as a comprehensive list of projects prioritized for funding, ensuring they align 
with federal requirements.  

The State of Hawai‘i Capital Improvement Plan (CIP) budget complements the STIP by detailing the 
specific funding allocated for infrastructure projects within the state. While the STIP focuses on the broader 
program of transportation improvements, the CIP provides the financial framework necessary for project 
implementation, including revenue sources, expenditures, and timelines.

The STIP and the CIP may not fully align due to differences in their planning horizons, funding sources, and 
project prioritization processes (see Figure 2). The STIP only includes federally-funded state and county 
projects and must adhere to federal guidelines, while the CIP encompasses a broader range of state projects 
that may not qualify for federal support.

The decision to construct infrastructure that increases, rather than decreases, vehicle travel is important. An 
individual’s transportation behavior depends on the type of public infrastructure available for use. Generally

encourage alternative modes of transportation, such as walking, biking, rolling and taking transit.

In addition to federal funding, the State of Hawaiʻi generates funding for transportation projects identified in 
the annual CIP through several mechanisms, including: 

•  General Obligation (GO) Bonds and GO Reimbursable Bonds: These bonds are municipal bonds backed by 
the full faith and credit of the state, and are typically repaid through state taxes. GO Reimbursable Bonds 
can be repaid from both specific revenue sources and general taxation. GO Reimbursable Bonds often fund 
projects that can generate their own revenue to cover bond payments. 

•  Revenue Bonds: These bonds are repaid solely from the income generated by the specific projects or 

sources funded by the bonds, without reliance on general taxation or credit of the issuer.  

• 

Special Funds: These funds are distinct revenue sources that are dedicated to specific transportation 
projects and programs. The revenue source is typically related to the projects or programs it is funding.

The SRTS Fund was first established by Act 317 (2012), which created county-level SRTS programs in addition to 
a state program.34 The revenue source for the SRTS Fund are traffic violation surcharges.35 From FY20 to FY23,

appropriated from the State Highway Fund into the SRTS Fund in FY24 and FY25.37

In addition to allocating a meager 1.5 percent to pedestrian/bike infrastructure, HDOT’s CIP budget for FY19 to 
FY24 did not allocate any funding to transit projects. Moreover, where pedestrian/bike infrastructure projects did 
exist, they often were in the form of widened roadway shoulders, which offer among the lowest form of protection 
for pedestrians, bicyclists and other vulnerable roadway users. Comparatively, HDOT allocated 42.3 percent of its 
CIP budget to new roadway capacity projects that introduce new vehicle lanes and/or roadway connections. 

Roadway expansion projects can significantly increase a region's vehicle travel. For example, in 2022 the 
Hawaiʻi Climate Change Commission analyzed the long-term impacts of nine major roadway expansion projects 
planned and prioritized by HDOT. The commission found that these nine projects, representing 38 new miles of 
roadway, would increase regional vehicle travel by 144 million miles, thus adding an additional 11,100 vehicles 
on the road and increasing greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions by 1.65 million tons.38 

Generally speaking, once roadway expansion projects are installed, vehicle travel increases are locked in, 
unless and until there is work to undue the increased demand impacts, such as road congestion charges or 
reallocation of the roadway for other travel modes.

These findings, which indicate that projects that increase vehicle travel represent a majority of the state’s

RECOMMENDATIONS

To address the economic, social and environmental challenges caused by rising vehicle travel, the state must 
shift away from its current transportation budgeting approach that prioritizes the planning and construction 
of vehicle infrastructure and instead develop transportation investment strategies that accommodate and 
encourage alternative modes of transportation. To accomplish this goal, Hawaiʻi Appleseed makes the 
following recommendations: 

1. Establish new budget priorities that reflect required vehicle travel reduction 
goals

As part of the Navahine agreement, HDOT is required to develop a GHG Reduction Plan to decarbonize the 
state’s transportation sector by 2045. The GHG Reduction Plan must include performance metrics to reduce 
vehicle travel and expand multimodal transportation options. A critical component to achieving these 
forthcoming goals is establishing new budget priorities that prioritize the reduction of vehicle travel. 

As discussed in this report, HDOT’s current project prioritization guidance gives precedent to roadway 
expansion projects over non-vehicular infrastructure. In alignment with the Navahine agreement, Hawaiʻi 
Appleseed recommends that HDOT establish new criteria for prioritizing projects within its budget to achieve 
vehicle travel reduction goals. This would likely include identifying a minimum threshold of HDOT’s budget that 
should be allocated to projects that reduce vehicle travel, and maximum threshold of the budget that can be 
allocated to projects that increase vehicle travel. 

In this effort, special attention should also be given to budgeting procedures to prevent new roadway 
capacity projects, given the particularly harmful impacts that new roads and lanes can pose. If HDOT is to 
pursue roadway capacity projects, they should be required to provide justification for the project and outline 
mitigation efforts. 

2. Increase budget transparency by disclosing vehicle travel impacts of 
proposed projects and actively prioritize projects that reduce vehicle travel

HDOT does not currently quantify or track impacts to vehicle travel for projects that are prioritized in its 
budget. Quantifying impacts to vehicle travel is a critical step to ensure that the state is on track to meet its 
climate goals. Hawaiʻi Appleseed recommends that HDOT develop a scientifically-based methodology for 
quantifying impacts to vehicle travel, and that the agency incorporate this methodology into future planning 
and budgeting. 

Within these processes, HDOT should also develop a system to prioritize projects that reduce vehicle travel. 

20 • RETHINKING ROADS

Moreover, to ensure full transparency, information about projects being prioritized for funding, including 
information about their impact to vehicle travel, should be publicly available in an accessible online format. To 
ensure that projects are achieving their stated goals, these systems should include an evaluation mechanism 
that tracks project impacts following implementation. As documented in the Navahine agreement, these 
procedures should be in place for the next federal funding planning cycle beginning in April, 2025.

3. Increase the amount of dedicated funding for the SRTS Fund to support the 
completion of the state’s pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure network

The Navahine agreement mandates that HDOT complete its multimodal network within 5 years. This includes 
the construction of planned pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure, as well as transit networks. This momentous 
undertaking will require a significant investment. The Hawaiʻi Climate Commission identified nearly $1 billion 
in high-priority pedestrian, bicycle, and multimodal projects across the state (including both county and state 
projects) that are currently unfunded.40 Implementing the state’s share of bike facilities on Oʻahu alone will cost 
$168 million.41

One of the many challenges to this effort is the state’s current lack of dedicated funding for alternative 
modes of transportation. As discussed earlier in this report, the SRTS Fund is one of the few funding sources 
that is specified for this purpose. While $10 million was allocated into this fund in FY24 and FY25, the SRTS 
Fund is typically severely underfunded, as it relies on traffic violation surcharges for its revenue. Since FY20, 
approximately $500,000 per year has been deposited into the SRTS Fund. Hawaiʻi Appleseed recommends 
increasing the dedicated annual funding source to the SRTS Fund to at least $15 million per year. 

One potential pathway to achieve this outcome is to appropriate a certain portion (approximately 5 percent) 
of the State Highway Fund to the SRTS Fund on an annual basis. In 2023 alone, the State Highway Fund saw 
revenue of approximately $316 million.42 By utilizing monies from the State Highway Fund, which relies upon 
state fuel taxes, vehicle weight taxes, and rental/tour vehicle taxes for revenue, those who benefit from 
publicly-funded vehicle infrastructure can contribute to infrastructure and amenities for our most vulnerable 
roadway users who rely on pedestrian, bicycle and transit infrastructure.
