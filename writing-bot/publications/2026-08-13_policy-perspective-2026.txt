Hawaiʻi Appleseed is committed to a more socially and economically just Hawaiʻi, where everyone has genuine 
opportunities to achieve economic security and fulfill their potential. We change systems to address inequity 
and foster greater opportunity by conducting data analysis and research to address income inequality, educating 
policymakers and the public, engaging in collaborative problem solving and coalition building, and advocating 
for policy and systems change.

The work of Hawaiʻi Appleseed is about people. The issues we work on—housing, food, wages, mobility, the state 
budget and taxation, and racial and indigenous equity—are important because they ensure people have access 
to shelter, sustenance, and the means to survive and thrive individually and collectively. Addressing these issues 
requires the knowledge and expertise of the people that have first-hand experience and live with the adverse 
consequences of our flawed systems.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Members of the Hawaiʻi Tax Fairness Coalition 
are recognized by Senator Karl Rhoads while 
attending a Hawaiʻi State Senate floor session 
in March 2026. // Will Caron, Hawaiʻi Appleseed 

THE 2026 LEGISLATIVE SESSION in Hawaiʻi was a study in tension—a session in which the urgent rhetoric around 
affordability, equity, and sustainability met the hard reality of political will and fiscal choices. 

For Hawaiʻi Appleseed, the session yielded a mixed ledger: real, tangible victories in food access and 
transportation equity; a significant shift in tax policy; but also profound disappointments where transformative, 
evidence-based reforms stalled due to entrenched interests and a failure of political courage.

This year’s Policy in Perspective report does more than catalog wins and losses. It analyzes why certain policy 
outcomes emerged the way they did, identifying a common thread across issue areas: a systemic reluctance to 
challenge the status quo. 

The most significant failures of the session—on tax fairness, on housing reform, and on pedestrian safety—all 
share a root cause. They are reforms that would rebalance power or resources away from those with established 
political influence, and toward those with unmet needs: renters, Native Hawaiian beneficiaries, low-income 
families, and young people.

Tax Fairness: Pausing the Cuts but Halting the Vision

The session on tax and budget policy reflected a significant ideological battleground. The passage of Senate Bill 
3125, which partially paused Act 46’s planned tax cuts for high earners, was a critical victory. It represented a 
necessary corrective, recognizing that the state could not afford to bleed $7 billion in revenue over five years while 
federal safety nets were being slashed. 

New leadership in the House Finance committee went a step further, raising the income tax rate on households 
that earn over $1 million. This decision demonstrated a growing, albeit cautious, willingness to ask the wealthiest 
Hawaiʻi residents to contribute their fair share.

However, the broader “Fund Our Future” vision of the Hawaiʻi Tax Fairness Coalition—to create a truly equitable 
tax system—remained largely unfulfilled. Bold proposals to close the capital gains tax loophole, tax investment 
income like work, or to close the corporate tax loopholes did not cross the finish line. 

The state’s budget, a reflection of its values, still relies disproportionately on the regressive General Excise Tax, 
which hits low-income families hardest. The failure to build on the momentum of the millionaire’s income tax to 
create a more fundamentally just and stable revenue structure leaves the state’s long-term fiscal health and its 
ability to invest in communities in a precarious position. 

The session’s outcome on taxes suggests a willingness to take incremental steps, but not the courage to 
fundamentally reform a system that perpetuates inequality.

The Political Economy of Housing Stagnation

The gap between legislative rhetoric and legislative action was most pronounced in housing. Lawmakers 
repeatedly declared housing a top priority, yet the reforms most directly tied to lowering costs—those that 
challenge the interests of existing property owners—failed to advance. 

4 • POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE

The analysis of the session reveals a clear pattern. Legislation aimed at eliminating parking mandates, which 
add over $68,000 to the cost of a single affordable rental unit, was repeatedly stalled. Bills intended to limit 
local discretion over accessory dwelling units (ADUs) and “missing middle” housing were weakened, leaving the 
operational power in the hands of counties and, by extension, vocal hyper-local interests. 

A similar dynamic was at play in the pursuit of a dedicated funding stream for the Department of Hawaiian Home 
Lands. The defeat of the conveyance tax reform bill (Senate Bill 3028), which would have provided up to $60 
million annually to help clear a waitlist of nearly 30,000 Native Hawaiian families, represents a foundational failure 
of the state’s constitutional and fiduciary duty. 

While the Legislature allocated some funding to DHHL projects, the refusal to establish a permanent revenue source 
leaves the agency’s long-term planning crippled and its future reliant on the whims of annual budget battles. 

Also left behind: a bill to require plain-language, multilingual landlord-tenant code summaries for tenants. This 
simple reform required no policy changes—just a commitment to accessibility.

Food Wins and Transit Near-Misses

In contrast to the gridlock on housing and comprehensive tax reform, the 2026 session delivered meaningful 
victories in food security and made important, if incomplete, progress on transportation equity.

The food policy agenda saw the most significant wins. The passage of Senate Bill 3245, which simplifies the 
SNAP recertification process for kūpuna, and House Bill 1518, which creates a pre-release application process for 
incarcerated individuals, are landmark achievements. 

These laws are not just administrative tweaks; they are a recognition that food is a basic need that should not 
be held hostage by burdensome bureaucracy or punitive criminal justice policies. These wins were the result of 
persistent, multi-year advocacy and a clear demonstration of the power of coalition building.

This session also saw a notable surge in community engagement on transportation equity. The “Summer Streets” 
pilot program and the legal clarity provided by the e-bike classification bill are important, albeit modest, steps 
toward a more multi-modal transportation system. They represent a shift in the conversation, however small, 
away from a purely car-centric model.

Yet, in a familiar pattern, the most transformative transportation bills were stopped at the finish line. Pedestrian 
Head Start, a low-cost measure proven to dramatically reduce roadway fatalities, and a pair of modest jaywalking 
reform bills, were allowed to die in conference committee without a public explanation or recorded vote. 

The failure to pass Keiki Ride Free—a program that would connect youth to opportunity and ease the burden 
on working families—was justified on cost, even as the state continues to spend billions on expanding vehicle 
capacity. The data on resource allocation is stark: between 2019 and 2024, 66 percent of HDOT’s budget went 
toward projects that expand vehicle capacity, while only 1.5 percent went toward reducing it. This reveals a 
systemic prioritization that the budget makes plain. 

The failure on these fronts comes down to a lack of willingness to challenge the dominant car culture and 
reallocate resources away from it.

Key Lessons: The Power of Honesty and Persistent Advocacy

The 2026 session offers several key lessons for Hawaiʻi Appleseed and our partners.

• 

First, evidence is not enough. While our rigorous, data-driven research is the foundation of our work, the 
outcomes of this session show that political will, not evidence, is the binding constraint. The reforms that 
failed—parking mandates, conveyance tax reform, pedestrian head starts—all have overwhelming evidence of 
their efficacy and are operating successfully in peer jurisdictions. Their failure is a failure of political courage to 
challenge powerful, entrenched interests with ready access to key decision-makers.

•  Second, the power of the status quo is formidable. The legislative process is designed to be deliberative, 

but it is also structurally biased against change. The conference committee once again became a graveyard for 
ambitious bills, often without a clear explanation or vote. This lack of transparency is itself a form of political 
power, one that favors the interests of those who benefit from the current system.

•  Third, incrementalism has limits but wins matter. While we missed the mark on transformative change in 
several areas, the wins in food security and the partial tax victory are not just consolation prizes. They are 
tangible improvements in people’s lives and they build a foundation for future progress. They demonstrate 
that sustained, multi-year advocacy can yield results.

• 

Finally, the need for a fundamental shift in the tax code is undeniable. The state’s budget is a zero-sum 
game. If we are to fund housing, transportation, food, healthcare, and education for all, we must be honest 
about the need to generate revenue from those who have profited the most. The conversation on tax fairness 
is only beginning.

The 2026 session was a session of partial progress and profound caution. The lessons are clear: we must continue 
to build our coalitions, sharpen our arguments, and be unflinching in our analysis. We must continue to demand 
not just that legislators talk about the issues, but that they act on them—and hold them accountable when they do 
not. Our vision for a just and equitable Hawaiʻi requires persistent, strategic advocacy that is unafraid to challenge 
the powerful. The work continues.

TAX & BUDGET

Hawaiʻi Appleseed executive director Will 
White speaks at a press conference for the 
Fund Our Future campaign in March 2026. 
// Will Caron, Hawaiʻi Appleseed

Hawaiʻi is confronting a perfect storm of pressures—diminishing state revenue, looming federal cuts, and the 
mounting costs of climate disaster recovery—that threaten to unravel the programs working families depend on. 
The 2026 legislative session made some progress toward a fairer tax system, but it won’t be enough to fill the 
widening gaps in our state budget. 

If we fail to act boldly in 2027, the consequences will be severe: more families priced out of their homes, more 
children going hungry, more Native Hawaiians waiting decades for land, and more communities devastated by 
preventable disasters.

The sources of budget strain are threefold and compounding. First, the income tax cuts enacted through Act 46 
of 2024 continue to drain state revenue.1 While SB3125—signed into law as Act 24—softened the blow by rolling 
back some of the tax cuts for the wealthy and adding a 2 percent surcharge on income over $1 million, the overall 

POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE • 7

damage remains. Even with these changes, Act 46 will still reduce state revenue by more than $1 billion annually 
by 2031—around a 10th of Hawaiʻi’s total tax revenue. 

Second, at the federal level, H.R. 1—the “One Big Beautiful Bill”—extends the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act while 
cutting safety net programs to pay for it.2 Hawaiʻi faces hundreds of millions in funding losses, with Medicaid and 
SNAP bearing the brunt. 

The state could be forced to cover up to $700 million in annual costs just to maintain current services. If unable 
to do so, an estimated 19,000–38,000 Hawaiʻi residents could lose their Medicaid coverage, and thousands could 
lose SNAP benefits.3 These programs are lifelines for working families and kūpuna. As Governor Green and our 
executive director Will White wrote in a joint op-ed, “We cannot continue to cut income taxes while federal funds 
disappear—not when schools, health care and infrastructure hang in the balance.”4

Third, climate disasters are no longer hypothetical. The Kona Low storms of early 2026 caused at least $1 billion 
in damage across the islands,5 demonstrating that extreme weather is already here. Flooding, landslides, and 
infrastructure failures disrupted communities and strained emergency services. 

As sea levels rise and storm patterns intensify, Hawaiʻi will face increasingly frequent and costly disasters, 
requiring more revenue for recovery and adaptation.6 The state’s failure to invest proactively in resilience will only 
drive costs higher in the long run.

The consequences of this fiscal squeeze are already visible in the state budget.7 The Department of Hawaiian 
Home Lands saw its funding drop from $185 million in fiscal year 2026 to $131 million in 2027—this at a time when 
the department faces an estimated $800 million shortfall and over 29,000 Native Hawaiian beneficiaries remain on 
the homestead waiting list.8

The Office on Homelessness and Housing Solutions saw its funding cut from $25 million to $18.6 million, even as 
our housing crisis deepens. These cuts are not abstractions; they represent real families left without homes, real 
communities left without support. 

Yet the revenue options to address these challenges are well understood and readily available:9 

• 

Taxing capital gains at the same rate as ordinary income would generate at least $85 million annually, with 88 
percent of the new revenue coming from the top 1 percent of earners.10 

•  Modernizing the conveyance tax—which hasn’t been updated since 2009—could provide up to $300 million 

a year for housing, infrastructure, and the general fund, while actually lowering taxes for the vast majority of 
local homebuyers.11

•  Closing corporate loopholes would ensure that multi-billion-dollar corporations pay their fair share, just as 

local small businesses already do.12

The 2027 legislative session will be decisive. The revenue is available, and the moral imperative is clear. 
What is missing is political will. But there is reason for hope. States across the country—Maine, Washington, 
Massachusetts—are asking their wealthiest residents to pay a little more, recognizing that a fairer tax code isn’t 
about punishment but about common sense.13 

Hawaiʻi’s leaders have shown they can take incremental steps. Now they must show they have the courage to 
do what is necessary. The alternative—continued cuts to the programs that keep our families housed, fed, and 
healthy—is not a future we can afford.

Fund Our Future: A Campaign for Tax Fairness 

In March 2026, a broad alliance of community organizations, labor unions, nonprofit advocates, and 
concerned residents gathered at the State Capitol in support of the Fund Our Future campaign—a 
movement dedicated to reforming Hawaiʻi’s tax system so that the wealthiest residents and large 
corporations pay their fair share, generating revenue for critical community needs without burdening 
working families.

The coalition’s message was clear: with Hawaiʻi facing pressing needs in housing, education, healthcare, 
and climate adaptation, the answer lies not in cutting services, but in fixing a broken tax code where 
the wealthiest residents and large corporations currently pay a smaller share of their income than many 
working families.

Coalition members highlighted four key policy proposals that would begin to correct this imbalance. On 
pausing income tax cuts for wealthy households, Nikos Leverenz of the Hawaiʻi Health & Harm Reduction 
Center emphasized: “The federal government is cutting taxes for the rich and slashing funding for 
Medicaid, SNAP, and other lifelines. If we proceed with additional state tax cuts on top of that, we will be 
forced to gut the very programs working families rely on.”

On taxing investment income like income from work, Younghee Overly of Indivisible Hawaiʻi made a 
personal plea: “As a retiree who depends on capital gains income, my message to the Legislature is 
simple: tax me more. The real threat to our democracy is not a modest tax increase on investment 
income—it is the unchecked accumulation of wealth that allows a few to wield power over the many.”

On closing the Real Estate Investment Trust loophole, Tanya Yamanaka Aynessazian of the Chamber of 
Sustainable Commerce called out the unfairness: “REITs are massive corporations that pay zero state 
income tax—zero—while every local business the Chamber represents pays their fair share. It’s wrong, 
and it’s time to close this loophole.”

And on raising the conveyance tax, ʻAlihilani Katoa of Our Hawaiʻi connected the fight to the promise of a 
Hawaiʻi where future generations can afford to stay: “The conveyance tax is one of our best tools to make 
sure that those profiting from our red-hot real estate market actually contribute something back to the 
communities they’re cashing in on. That’s not just good policy. That’s keeping our promise.”

The Fund Our Future campaign organized and mobilized throughout the remainder of the 2026 session, 
urging lawmakers to prioritize tax fairness and community investment over continued breaks for the 
wealthy and corporations. While the coalition secured a partial victory with the passage of SB3125, the 
broader vision remains unfinished—and the work continues.

AFFORDABLE HOUSING

Apartments in Salt Lake, Oʻahu. // 
Will Caron, Hawaiʻi Appleseed

Year after year, lawmakers identify housing as a top priority. They rightly note that the cost of living is forcing 
families out; that our kids can no longer afford to stay once they are grown. Renters,14 working families,15 Native 
Hawaiian households,16 and young people trying to enter the market all bear the weight of a gap between what 
they earn and what a home costs—a gap that has never been wider.17

Despite the overwhelming recognition of the problem among legislators, the 2026 legislative session delivered 
yet another chapter in a frustrating pattern: Reforms most directly linked to lowering housing costs stalled or died 
quietly, often at the hands of the same legislators who just identified housing as their top concern. 

The solutions are not mysterious. They are operating in peer jurisdictions right now, producing measurable 
results. They do not require new taxes or federal coordination. They largely require repealing rules that our own 
state and local governments created. What is missing is honesty about whom these reforms actually threaten and 
why bills with broad public support keep failing for reasons that rarely get named.

12 • POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE

Consider parking requirements. Most local codes force developers to build a set number of off-street parking 
spaces per residential unit, regardless of location, transit access, or actual demand. In urban Honolulu, podium 
parking adds more than $68,000 to the cost of a single affordable rental unit—roughly $410 per month in extra rent 
for a studio, whether the tenant owns a car or not.18 

The fix is simple: end the mandate. Minneapolis,19 Buffalo,20 and California have all acted, eliminating or reducing 
parking minimums without the chaos opponents predicted. In Hawaiʻi, legislation that acknowledges the problem 
has repeatedly failed.

A similar pattern appears with accessory dwelling units and missing middle 
housing. California paired its ADU reform with binding rules: no parking 
requirements in many locations, strict permit timelines, and limited local 
design review. The result was a surge in ADU permits from under 5,000 in 
2017 to over 23,000 in 2022.21

Hawaiʻi chose the opposite approach. Our laws say ADUs are legal statewide, 
but hand counties the tools—infrastructure determinations, design 
guidelines, parking requirements—to reject projects one lot at a time. The 
gap between promise and reality is an intentional choice. 

Auckland upzoned its entire city22 and saw rents grow slower than in peer 
cities.23 Minneapolis eliminated single-family zoning.24 Oregon mandated 
missing middle permissions statewide.25 In Hawaiʻi, we continue to protect 
single-family-exclusive development across most of our urban residential land.

Missing middle housing 
refers to a range of multi-
unit or clustered housing 
types—such as duplexes, 
triplexes, fourplexes, 
townhomes, and 
courtyard apartments—
that are compatible in 
scale with single-family 
homes but offer higher, 
walkable densities.

The political calculus is familiar. Existing homeowners vote. Future residents, young renters, families priced out, 
workers who have already left—these groups do not yet exist as a constituency. Every year parking mandates 
remain in place, every year missing middle housing remains illegal on most urban land, and every year ADU 
permitting remains exposed to local overrides, the cost of housing continues to outpace the incomes of the people 
who need it most.

The failure to provide a dedicated funding stream for the Department of Hawaiian Home Lands represents another 
profound missed opportunity. More than 100 years ago, Congress passed the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act 
of 1921—a promise to set aside 200,000 acres of land for Native Hawaiians. Today, nearly 30,000 Native Hawaiian 
families sit on a waitlist. Most have waited decades. Too many have died waiting. 

The department has been chronically underfunded, forced to beg the Legislature for money annually with no 
guaranteed source. SB3028 would have given DHHL a permanent, dedicated revenue stream of up to $60 million 
per year by restructuring the state’s conveyance tax. The bill would convert Hawaiʻi’s flat rate—laughably low at 
between 0.1–1.25 percent of a transaction’s value—to a marginal rate system. 

This change would have reduced taxes for 94 percent of local buyers, while raising taxes on luxury property sales. 
A middle-class family selling their median Oʻahu home would pay less than they do now, while the seller of a $25 
million beachfront property would pay meaningfully more. The estimated additional revenue: more than $100 
million annually.26

The bill died in conference committee. Opponents raised concerns that even the $2 million threshold could make 
homes more expensive for local buyers. Lawmakers considered a last-minute compromise raising the threshold to 
$4 million and applying higher rates to non-owner-occupied properties but couldn’t reach agreement on revenue

allocations. The result: another year without a permanent funding source for DHHL, another year of piecemeal 
appropriations, another year of broken promises to Native Hawaiian families.

The state currently dedicates more funding to incarceration than we do to the Department of Hawaiian Home 
Lands. This fiscal year, DHHL’s budget sits at $185 million, while we spend roughly $300 million on prisons.27 We’re 
investing more in jailing people—disproportionately Native Hawaiians, often for non-violent offenses—than we are 
in housing them. Instead of spending millions to lock people up, we should be investing in housing them. It would 
prevent crime, reduce homelessness, and honor our obligations. That we don’t already do this is an indictment of 
our policy priorities as a state.

The state also missed an opportunity to advance just cause eviction (JCE) legislation, which would establish clear 
legal standards for evictions and lease non-renewals.28 Current Hawaiʻi law allows landlords to terminate month-
to-month tenancies without providing a reason as long as they issue 45 days’ notice29—a loophole that allows 
landlords to evade retaliation protections and leaves tenants with the choice to endure unsafe conditions in 
silence or risk losing their housing. 

JCE laws have been successfully adopted in California, Oregon, Washington, and New York.30 They do not prevent 
legitimate evictions; they simply establish a clear standard between tenants displaced through no fault of their 
own and those who have violated lease terms.31 For responsible tenants, JCE provides security from arbitrary 
displacement. For landlords, it provides a clear, predictable legal process. By codifying JCE, Hawaiʻi could create a 
more fair and stable housing market for everyone.

Another missed opportunity: a bill requiring landlords to provide tenants with a plain-language, non-English 
translation of the landlord-tenant code. It wouldn’t have changed the law—just made it accessible.

What is missing is not knowledge or policy options. What is missing is the political will to challenge the interests 
that benefit from the status quo. Other states have acted. The question is whether our leaders will stop talking 
about housing and start doing the same.

FOOD EQUITY

Gov. Green, the First Lady and 
advocates gather for the House Bill 
2296 signing ceremony, July 2026. // 
Office of the Governor

The 2026 legislative session delivered another chapter of steady, if incomplete, progress in Hawaiʻi’s fight against 
food insecurity. As the cost of living crisis tightens its grip on families across the islands, lawmakers demonstrated 
a growing recognition that food security is not a peripheral concern but a central pillar of community well-being. 

Following multiple federal funding crises,32 growing threats to nutrition assistance programs,33 and last year’s food 
security victories,34 the state legislature could not ignore the mounting pressures that Hawaiʻi families face.

Yet the numbers remain stark. One in three local families still struggles to put food on the table.35 The people who 
grow our food, harvest it, and put it on our tables are going hungry themselves—food system workers are roughly 
two and a half times more likely to face food insecurity than workers in other sectors.36 

The devastating Kona Low storms that destroyed over 400 homes37 and caused more than $42 million in 
documented agricultural losses38 have laid bare just how fragile our food system remains.39 Only about 15 percent 
of our food is grown locally.40 This is not a collection of separate problems. It is one broken system.

Against this backdrop, the 2026 session delivered meaningful victories. After years of advocacy, lawmakers 
finally passed Senate Bill 3245, which simplifies the SNAP recertification process for kūpuna, extending 
certification periods from one year to two years and requiring state participation in the Elderly Simplified 
Application Project (ESAP). 

POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE • 17

Kalo growing in Heʻeia, 
Oʻahu. // Will Caron, 
Hawaiʻi Appleseed

The legislature also passed House Bill 1518, a landmark collaboration between anti-hunger and civil rights 
advocates that creates a pre-release SNAP application process for incarcerated individuals. Starting in 2028, those 
nearing release will receive their EBT card the moment they re-enter society, closing a gap that can mean the 
difference between reintegration and recidivism.

Investments in local food systems also saw a bright spot. Lawmakers appropriated an additional $898,000 for 
the Farm to Families program through the state budget (House Bill 1800)—building on last year’s initial $500,000 
allocation—allowing food banks to purchase and distribute nearly $1.4 million worth of Hawaiʻi-grown food every 
year. Every dollar spent on local food procurement keeps dollars circulating in our economy while strengthening 
our agricultural resilience. 

In another small but significant win, Governor Green signed House Bill 2296 into law, lowering the required charge 
for school meals from half to one-quarter of production cost—a technical change that will provide meaningful 
financial relief to working families already stretched thin by the state’s high cost of living.

The 2025 expansion of free school meals under Act 139 marked a major step forward, with an additional 11,000 
public school students gaining access this coming fall.41 However, lawmakers in 2026 failed to build on that 
momentum. Children just over the income cliff, public charter school students, and families who struggle with 
paperwork remain left behind.42 Despite Governor Green’s five-year vision for universal free school meals, the 
legislature did not fund an expansion to all students by 2030.

18 • POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE

The federal government is not coming to the rescue. The effects of federal cuts to nutrition assistance have 
become clearer, and the recent 43-day government shutdown offered a troubling preview of what a world 
without consistent SNAP benefits could look like.43 The legislature did appropriate $14.2 million for food banks 
and the Hawaiʻi Emergency Food Assistance Program, along with $16.5 million to supplement health insurance 
access following the expiration of Affordable Care Act premiums through House Bill 2310. But these are stopgap 
measures, not long-term solutions.

What this experience has shown is that Hawaiʻi is capable of stepping up to care for our people—even in the 
absence of federal support. The wins this session are worth celebrating, but they are not the finish line. One in 
three households still struggles with food insecurity. Federal cuts will only increase pressure on state and local 
programs in the years ahead. 

Hawaiʻi has an opportunity to build a more resilient, community-driven food system—one that protects families 
regardless of federal uncertainty. The challenge now is to build on this momentum and finish what we started.

EQUITY

The Pilina Builders meet in 
Waipahu. // Christy MacPherson, 
Hawaiʻi Appleseed

Hawaiʻi’s pedestrians are in crisis. Fatalities are rising, dangerous intersections remain unfixed, and the people 
most affected—keiki, kūpuna, people with disabilities, and low-income families who walk out of necessity—
continue to bear the heaviest burden.44 

Transportation costs are also quietly draining household budgets. It is, on average, the third largest household 
expense in Honolulu, and low-income families spend an average of 30 percent of their after-tax income on 
transportation.45 The public cost of our car-dependent system is staggering: an estimated $33,600 per household 
per year, regardless of whether they own a vehicle.46

POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE • 21

These challenges are compounded by a looming federal funding crisis. Recent shifts in federal spending threaten 
more than $131 million in transportation funds for Hawaiʻi—$91.25 per resident.47 Executive orders have frozen 
key multimodal infrastructure programs, including pedestrian walkways, bike infrastructure, and transit projects. 

Programs like Thriving Communities and Reconnecting Communities have been paused, while new policies 
prioritize car-dependent communities and suspend the bicycle commuter benefit and EV tax credit. With nearly 
half of HDOT’s capital budget reliant on federal dollars, the state can no longer count on Washington to fund the 
multimodal infrastructure residents desperately need. If Hawaiʻi wants safer streets and affordable transit, the 
state must step in and fill the gap.

Yet the 2026 legislative session demonstrated once again that the state’s transportation priorities remain 
misaligned with both its climate commitments and the needs of working families. Between fiscal year 2019–2024, 
66 percent of the Hawaiʻi Department of Transportation (HDOT)’s $5.68 billion budget went toward projects 
that expand vehicle capacity.48 Only 1.5 percent was directed toward reducing vehicle travel through alternative 
transportation.49 This is not a funding shortage—it is a choice.

The most promising pedestrian safety measures advanced further than ever before, only to die quietly at the 
finish line. The Pedestrian Head Start bill (SB2470)—which would have required installation of Leading Pedestrian 
Intervals (LPIs) and Accessible Pedestrian Signals (APS) at intersections statewide—cleared its committee hearings 
and made it to conference, but no conferees were ever assigned. 

The evidence for these low-cost interventions is overwhelming:50 Seattle saw a 48 percent reduction in pedestrian 
collisions with LPIs; New York City saw a 65 percent drop in fatal daytime crashes. Yet in Hawaiʻi, the bill simply 
expired without explanation. 

The same fate befell jaywalking reform—a pair of modest bills that would have reduced fines from up to $180 to 
$25 (HB1523) and removed countdown timer violations (HB1524). More than 5,000 jaywalking citations are issued 
annually, concentrated in working-class neighborhoods where residents walk out of necessity.51 For families living 
paycheck to paycheck, these fines are not minor inconveniences—they can spiral into debt and court records. 

The pattern is unmistakable: both bills reached conference and in both cases, the Senate did not assign conferees. 
There was no floor debate, no recorded vote, no public explanation.52 

Meanwhile, the Legislature also failed to advance Keiki Ride Free (HB1879), a program that would cost just $9.87 
million annually53—less than 1 percent of HDOT’s five-year budget—and would provide fare-free transit for all 
youth, saving families hundreds to thousands of dollars per year while reducing emissions.54 Also killed this year, 
Subsidized Transit for State Employees (SB2993), and Free Transit for All (HB2451).

Despite these setbacks, there were modest wins worth celebrating. The Summer Streets pilot program (SB3029) 
will temporarily close roads to cars and open them for pedestrians and cyclists, giving communities a lived 
experience of what streets could feel like with less emphasis on automobiles.55 

The e-bike classification bill (HB2021) finally provides legal clarity for a mode of transportation that has 
become essential for many working families and young people, establishing clear rules and preventing 
uneven enforcement.56 

But these are starting points, not finish lines. The Summer Streets pilot’s impact depends entirely on county 
implementation. The e-bike bill provides a framework, not funding for infrastructure. And the Safe Routes to School 

22 • POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE

program, established with community-driven processes in 2023, faces new threats from legislation (SB3234) that 
would allow HDOT to bypass its transparent evaluation process and direct funds to its own projects.57

The landmark Navahine v. HDOT settlement legally binds the state to decarbonize its transportation sector by 
2045.58 Lawmakers have both a legal obligation and a generational responsibility to cut emissions.59 They also 
have an opportunity to confront one of the most persistent drivers of Hawaiʻi’s high cost of living. 

The reforms that would make streets safer and transit more accessible are not experimental—they are operating 
in peer jurisdictions right now, producing measurable results.60 What is missing is not knowledge, evidence, or 
available policy options. What is missing is the political will to prioritize people over cars.

Pilina Builders: Community Leadership in Action

Transportation equity is about ensuring that every resident, regardless of income or zip code, can fully 
participate in the life of our islands. That belief sits at the heart of our work—and it’s why we are so 
excited to share the story of the Pilina Builders.

In 2025, Hawaiʻi Appleseed received an 18-month grant from the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation to 
support community-led transportation advocacy in Waipahu and ʻEwa. A core piece of this project is 
developing the leadership skills of a self-selected cohort of women from these communities. They have 
fittingly named themselves the Pilina Builders.

The name could not be more perfect for these 10 wāhine. Guided by the ʻōlelo noʻeau, Ma ka hana 
ka ʻike (in working one learns), they are a diverse, bright, and community-grounded group. Most are 
mothers, some are public transit users, and several already have leadership experience but are hungry 
to learn more.

The program launched in September 2025 with eight training sessions. The cohort learned the art of the 
“one-on-one”—a purposeful version of our local “talk story” way of communicating—and how it’s used 
to identify potential leaders. They reviewed community organizing and dove into discussions on land 
use, zoning, and transportation policy around Transit-Oriented Development. They even invited State 
Senator Rachele Lamosao and Honolulu City Councilmember Augie Tulba to talk story about making 
their communities better.

After graduating in December, the Pilina Builders hosted two community listening sessions in Waipahu 
and ʻEwa. Over 20 new residents attended each session—many for the first time. Because rail is coming 
through their communities, residents spoke passionately about connectivity, safety, and accessibility. 
Their insights were sharp, their ideas fresh, and their energy contagious.

Next, the Pilina Builders will invite participants to form working groups, prioritize the issues identified, 
and bring one or two more people to each meeting. The cohort is not just developing their own 
leadership skills—they are building them in others. This is how people power grows. This summer, they 
will engage public officials to introduce or change policies that address their community’s needs.
