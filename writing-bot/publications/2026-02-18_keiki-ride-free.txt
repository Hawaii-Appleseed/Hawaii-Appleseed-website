EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

PUBLIC TRANSIT is more than a way to get from place to place—it’s a tool for growth, opportunity and 
community connection. Transit is particularly important for young people, the largest demographic of residents 
who do not drive. 

When young people can move freely, they gain confidence, independence, and access to the full range of 
opportunities the islands offer. Transit connects keiki to after-school programs, internships, health care, 
recreation and friends, while also building lifelong habits that favor walking, biking and public transportation 
over driving alone.

To improve transit access, localities across the country are increasingly offering reduced and fare-free transit 
programs. This report examines the economic, health, and safety benefits of public transit; summarizes the 

KEIKI RIDE FREE • 3

current fare-free programs offered in Hawaiʻi; and discusses the costs and benefits of existing fare-free transit 
programs (local and national). A review of eight existing fare-free transit programs revealed several benefits of 
subsidizing transit fares. These include:

• 

Significant increases in overall transit ridership; 

•  Boosted local economic activity;

•  Reduced fuel consumption and carbon emissions; and

• 

Improved transit access for economically disadvantaged individuals and families. 

This report also provides recommendations to improve transit access and affordability, with a particular focus 
on young riders. The main recommendation is to implement a statewide, year-round fare-free transit program 
for residents age 18 and under. Similar to the free transit for youth provided in Washington State, this report 
recommends that the state legislature establish a dedicated state funding mechanism to provide county transit 
agencies recurring reimbursements for foregone youth fare revenue. 

To inform this policy report, our team relied on four key research methods: reviewing existing studies and reports 
on fare-free transit programs; examining the impacts of high transit fares on vulnerable community members; 
analyzing existing national and local fare-free and reduced-fare transit programs; and evaluating local  transit 
ridership trends.

BENEFITS OF PUBLIC 
TRANSIT

Public transit is an essential asset to rural communities and urban neighborhoods alike. Public transit is a 
lifeline for individuals who cannot drive or do not have a vehicle to access employment, education, and essential 
services. As roadway congestion and vehicle costs continue to grow, public transit has become more appealing 
for a variety of socioeconomic groups. Some of the key economic, health and safety benefits offered by public 
transit are summarized below. 

Figure 1: Benefits of Sustained Investment in Public Transit

1. ECONOMIC BENEFITS

Because 87 percent of all public transit trips directly contribute to the local economy, investment in public transit 
has been shown to spur economic growth.1 The American Public Transportation Association (APTA) reports that 
long-term transit investment yields strong economic benefits, with each $1 billion in additional annual spending 
generating roughly $5 billion in additional GDP over 20 years.2 A $1 billion annual investment in public transit has 
also been found to generate an estimated 17,900 jobs.3 

In 2025, the Ulupono Initiative found that the public cost of the vehicle economy4 in Hawaiʻi was $14,400 
per taxpayer, or $33,600 per household, regardless of vehicle ownership.5 The additional private cost of 
vehicle ownership is substantial—an extra $21,800 per household, per year.6 Given this, it is unsurprising that 
transportation is, on average, the second largest household expenditure in the U.S. In Honolulu, it is the third 
largest expenditure on average7 due to high local food costs (Figure 2, next page).

The cost of transportation is also regressive, as it consumes a larger share of household budgets for lower-
income residents than for higher-income ones.9 In 2022, the average U.S. household spent 16 percent of their 
after-tax income on transportation, while low-income households in the U.S. spent an average 30 percent of their 
after-tax income on transportation.10 

When individuals shift vehicle trips to transit trips, money saved from vehicle expenditures can be used for 
purchasing power on other necessities, such as housing, food and healthcare. If the U.S. were to sustain a 
higher level of investment in public transit for 20 years, the APTA estimates that would save American drivers an 
estimated $11.7 billion in annual in car operating costs.11

2. PUBLIC HEALTH BENEFITS 

Public transportation can improve public health through enhanced accessibility of health care services, 
reduction of carbon emission, and increased physical activity. In 2017, approximately 5.8 million people in the 
U.S. (1.8 percent) postponed medical care because they lacked access to transportation.12 According to a 2020 
study, medical care inaccessibility is most prominent for those below the federal poverty threshold, Medicaid 

6 • KEIKI RIDE FREE

  
recipients, as well as those with physical disabilities.13 This study highlighted the importance of communities 
having a variety of transportation options, including public transit, especially for economically-disadvantaged 
individuals as well as those with disabilities.14

Public transit often requires walking. One study found that 96 percent of people who use public transport 
walk from their starting point to a station or bus stop, and 95 percent walk from public transport to their final 
destination.15 An additional study found that adult transit users within the U.S. walked an average of 21 minutes 
daily to complete their trips.16 Transit users often achieve a majority of the daily recommendation of moderate 
physical activity for adults set by the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services in just their day to day lives 
without setting aside time to work out.17

3. ROAD SAFETY BENEFITS

Compared to vehicle travel, public transit is safer for its occupants, as well as pedestrians and cyclists. In 2018, a 
study comparing the safety of different roadway users on 10 major urban roads in Montreal found that the injury 
rate for personal vehicle occupants was 3.7 times higher than for bus occupants.18 The researchers found that 
pedestrian injury rates were 4.1 times higher along vehicle-dominated routes compared to bus routes.19 Overall, 
the study estimated that bus travel on these 10 routes alone prevented 2,437 injuries over a 10-year period.20

Local data tells a similar story. In 2024, the City and County of Honolulu’s bus fleet traveled over 21.6 million 
miles.21 That year there was an average of 553,145 miles per major bus collision—roughly 39 major bus collisions 
in total.22 In contrast, the Honolulu Police Department recorded 4,432 major vehicle collisions in 2024.23 

Each year, more than 100 people are killed in crashes on Hawaiʻi’s roads and highways, yet fatal bus crashes 
remain rare.24 From 2012 to 2018, an estimated 12 fatalities were associated with bus collisions.25 These figures 
suggest that shifting trips from personal vehicles to public transit could substantially reduce traffic injuries and 
fatalities, leading to fewer emergency room visits, lower long-term medical costs, and fewer families affected by 
serious crashes.

A. OVERALL RIDERSHIP

While public transit usage differs greatly between Hawaiʻi’s four counties, this section of the report focuses 
primarily on Oʻahu’s TheBus, since this system has the greatest number of riders, and the most data available. 
It is also the state’s most costly transit system. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, average monthly ridership on 
TheBus had decreased by nearly 10 percent between 2015 and 2019 (Figure 3). 27 During the COVID-19 pandemic 
TheBus reached an all-time low of approximately 30.4 million annual rides in 2021.28 

In 2022, annual ridership began to recover, with a 24.9 percent increase in ridership, compared to the dramatic 
decline in 2021. Between 2021 and 2025, total annual ridership continued to increase by an average of 9 percent 
annually.29 However, this positive trend has recently begun to decline. Between 2024 and 2025, total annual bus 
ridership increased by less than 1 percent.30

Honolulu’s ongoing fare hikes have likely contributed to its slow post-COVID ridership recovery. TheBus has seen 
two fare increases over the past five years, and in January 2026, the Honolulu City Council voted to pass Bill 
54. This bill will increase fares for adult, youth, and other reduced-fare riders (such as low-income, people with 
disabilities and kūpuna).31 Under Bill 54, both monthly and annual passes for youth will increase by 12.5 percent 
(Figure 4).32 

Increases to transit fares are particularly harmful for low-income riders who rely on it as their primary mode of 
transportation. Based on a 2021–2022 survey conducted by DTS, 21 percent of TheBus riders had a household 
annual income of less than $20,000, and 71 percent had a household income of less than $60,000.33 Survey 
results also showed that 54 percent of TheBus riders did not have a vehicle at home and 53 percent did not have 
a driver’s license.34

Increased fares come at a cost. Studies show that when transit fares increase, ridership declines.35 This dynamic 
is measured by “price elasticity of demand,” which describes how consumption or demand for a service changes 
when the prices change.36 In 2019, former DTS Director Wes Frysztacki informed the Honolulu Rate Commission 
that for every 10 percent increase in fare, there is a 3 percent decline in ridership.37

B. RIDERSHIP BY FARE CATEGORY

Of the roughly 42 million trips made on Oʻahu’s transit system in 2024, 26.2 percent of those trips (10.7 million) 
were made by reduced fare riders using a HOLO card (Figure 5). 38 Reduced fare seniors had the highest ridership 
of any reduced fare group (19 percent of total rides), and youth had the least, with only 391,520 rides.39 

While an estimated 20.2 percent of Oʻahu residents are below the age of 18,40 youth riders in 2024 accounted for 
less than 1 percent of public transit rides taken on Oʻahu. 

These figures for reduced-fare riders only account for individuals who used a HOLO card, and do not include 
those who paid for their transit fare via cash. To be eligible for a reduced fare, a rider must present a valid senior, 
disability, low-income, or youth HOLO Card. Any transit rider who pays cash must pay the full price. 

Youth ridership on Oʻahu’s public transit system is likely suppressed by fare costs and administrative barriers. 
While youth day fares are comparable to other reduced fare rates, monthly and annual passes are significantly 
more expensive for youth, and obtaining a youth HOLO card can be burdensome. These factors may discourage 
regular use and lead some youth to pay cash or avoid transit altogether. 

Findings from a late-2025 Hawaiʻi Appleseed travel survey underscore the potential impact of fare-free transit for 
youth. The survey reached 660 households across Oʻahu, and 36 percent of respondent households had school-
aged children.41 Among respondents with high school-aged children, 34 percent reported that their children

C. FARES VERSUS OPERATING COSTS

In 2024, the City and County of Honolulu’s public transit system operational costs were an estimated $330 
million.44 During this time period, approximately $47 million in passenger fares were collected, an operating loss 
of approximately $283 million, or an 85 percent deficit. A similar trend occurred in 2022 and 2023, with passenger 
fares covering less than 20 percent of operating costs during those years.45 

Opponents to reducing or eliminating transit fares often assume that fares are needed to cover operating costs. 
The reality is that in Honolulu, as in most localities, fare revenue covers only a small share of transit expenses. 
Local governments must fund the majority of transit operations regardless of fares.

LOCAL EXAMPLES

Hawaiʻi County, HI: The Hawaiʻi County Hele-On bus system launched a fare-free pilot program in 2022.46 The 
fare-free program allows anyone to use the bus without a transit pass.47 The program was promoted through the 
Hele-On website, signage on buses, and county press releases.

Following implementation of the fare-free program, in fiscal year (FY) 22 there were over 588,000 passenger rides 
across Hele-On’s buses, vans, and bikeshare services, representing an 81 percent increase from FY21.48 

In 2022, the Hawaiʻi County Council approved the extension of the free fare program through 2025.49 Grant 
funding from the Federal Transit Administration helped to cover the annual fare revenue loss of $560,000.50 
Hawaiʻi County Mass Transit Agency’s acting administrator, Zac Bergum, recently indicated that the agency 
would need to spend over $1 million if the county decided to reintroduce fares and install fare boxes throughout 
the Hele-On system.51

In 2025, the Hawaiʻi County Council approved the extension of the fare-free transit program through 2028.52 Part 
of the reasoning behind its extension has been the continual increase in ridership, which surpassed 940,000 
rides for FY25.53 Ridership now mirrors pre-pandemic levels, which was at an all-time high in 2012 (1.2 annual 
million rides).54 

▶

Lesson: Eliminating fares is an effective way to boost transit ridership to pre-pandemic levels.

Hawaiʻi Department of Education: HiDOE has faced an ongoing school bus driver shortage since the onset of 
the COVID-19 pandemic.55 Despite recruitment efforts such as providing signing bonuses, job fairs, and flexible 
schedules for drivers, the shortage has persisted.56

In 2022, HiDOE launched a pilot program called Expanding Ridership to Educate Students in Schools (EXPRESS). 
The EXPRESS program aimed to alleviate the impacts of Hawaiʻi’s school bus driver shortage and improve 
access to school by providing free county bus passes—only be used during the school year—to eligible high 
school students. 

Eligible students must apply through the HiDOE website, and will then receive a transit pass from their respective 
county. In 2022, it was estimated that the DOE spent $3–4 million on the pilot program.57 In 2025, the program 
was expanded to include intermediate school students. 

The program was originally available to all high school students without a school bus pass. However, to be 
eligible for the program currently, students must also live 1.5 miles or further from their school. Students who 
receive a geographic exemption are completely ineligible. The program also only serves students on Oʻahu and 
Kauaʻi, since Maui and Hawaiʻi County already provide free transit for youth.

In 2022, HIDOE estimated that the program could benefit 47 percent of high school students statewide.58 In the 
first year of the pilot program, 10,000 students enrolled in the program, with numbers  climbing as high as 17,600 
students in 2024.59 However, following the introduction of geographic restrictions, enrollment has since declined.

▶

Lesson: Providing free transit access can be a cost-effective way to improve access to school during a time 
that many students lack access to school transportation services. However, free transit programs that impose 
seasonal and geographic restrictions limit their impact.

NATIONAL EXAMPLES

Washington State: In 2021, Washington State enacted legislation enabling every transit system to offer free 
boarding for passengers under 18, including state ferries and Amtrak routes. This policy now provides free transit 
access to approximately 1.4 million young Washingtonians.60 

Officially launched in late 2022, the program allows Washington transit agencies to qualify for the newly-created 
Transit Support Grant, which reimburses agencies for the cost of lost fare revenue, and provides additional funds 
to invest in operations, capital and maintenance.61 To receive funding, transit agencies must document their 
implementation of the zero-fare policy, as well as provide ridership data.62 

Program funding is sustainable for the next 16 years through Washington’s Climate Commitment Act, distributed 
every two years to eligible transit agencies.63 For the 2023–2025 biennium, approximately $33.6 million from the 
Transit Support Grant was allocated to reimburse agencies for offering fare-free policy for youth.64

In 2023, Washington transit agencies reported more than 16.5 million fare-free rides for passengers under 
18, with most trips being taken on ground-based transit.65 After steep ridership declines during the COVID-19 
pandemic, the fare-free policy likely helped drive recovery. Between 2022 and 2023, total statewide transit trips 

16 • KEIKI RIDE FREE

increased by 19.8 percent, rising from 137.66 million in 2022 to 164.93 million in 2023.66 For agencies serving 
under 1 million passengers, total trips grew by 23.4 percent. 

Whatcom Smart Trips program manager Michelle Grandy further highlighted the program’s positive impact:

“Each year we find students to be more eager to ride the bus than the year before… Faces light up when 
suggested destinations are shared in our presentations. Photos of the waterslides, county fair, local arcade, 
and more spark the imagination of the students spurring good questions about where else the bus may go and 
how to find their closest bus stop. They’re especially excited when they learn the bus is free for them to ride.”67

The Washington State legislature recently allocated an additional $188.9 million for the Transit Support Grant 
for the 2025–2027 biennium, to be awarded to 31 transit agencies statewide.68 Individual awards will range from  
$864 to $66.1 million.69

▶

Lesson: Establishing a statewide policy for free transit provides riders universal transit access across 
different counties and transit systems (such as bus and rail). Establishing a statewide grant that provides 
reimbursements to county transit agencies is one potential way to accomplish this. 

Albuquerque, NM: ABQ Ride, Albuquerque’s public transportation system, previously charged adults $1 dollar 
for a one-way trip.70 In a 2017 rider survey, 84 percent of respondents reported annual household incomes below 
$35,000, and about 46% percent reported not owning a vehicle.71 As a result, being unable to afford transit fares 
was a frequent reality for much of ABQ Rides’s customer base.

In January 2022, the City of Albuquerque launched the ABQ Ride Zero Fares pilot, temporarily allowing anyone 
to ride ABQ Ride buses, ART lines, and Sun Van paratransit services (for qualified riders) for free. The pilot was 
funded by a one-time, non-recurring $3 million appropriation from the City Council, with the intent to continue 
or terminate based on data collected in 2022.72 Riders did not need to enroll, show ID, or use a transit pass to 
board the transit system; they could simply board and exit vehicles, and fareboxes were taken out of service.

ABQ Ride Zero Fare helped reverse the steep ridership declines caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Between 
December 2021 and December 2022, fixed-route ridership had increased by 49.4 percent73 and Sun Van 
paratransit ridership grew by 73 percent. On November 8, 2023, the City Council voted to make the zero-fare 
program permanent.74 In 2024, ABQ Ride provided over 7.3 million rides, reaching roughly 80 percent of pre-
pandemic annual ridership.75

During the first 12 months, the total cost of implementation, operations and maintenance for the program was 
just over $3 million.76 After the pilot funding was exhausted, ABQ Ride Zero Fare continued using the transit 
department’s regular budget. According to Transit Department leadership, fares had previously generated about 
$3 million per year in revenue in an agency with a $67 million annual budget, meaning fare revenue represented 
a small share of overall funding once collection and pass administration costs were considered.77 

If ABQ Ride had reinstituted fares and a pass system in 2023, it is estimated the city would face a net  revenue 
loss of about $1.8 million, roughly 3 percent of the Transit Department’s $58 million operating budget.78 By 
eliminating fare collection, funds once spent on fare administration are now redirected to improve service 
quality and to expand access to transit for Albuquerque residents.

▶

Lesson: Eliminating fares reduces administrative costs, allowing funds that would otherwise be spent on fare 
collection to go towards expanding transit access.

Alexandria, VA: In September 2021, Alexandria’s bus system, DASH, began offering free transit to all riders with 
no pass or other verification required. In FY22, the City of Alexandria contributed $1.47 million to subsidize the 
first 10 months of fare-free service.79 At the same time, DASH underwent a system redesign that introduced 15 
minute or less service intervals and a 24/7 bus system, reaching an additional 50,000 residents and 25,000 jobs.80 

Driven by the network redesign and the elimination of fares, average daily ridership between 2021 and 2023 
grew by over 152 percent (see Figure 8). 81A rider survey found that 53 percent of existing DASH riders reported 
increasing their transit use because of the free fare program. 

One challenge emerging from the program was overcrowding on frequent routes (running every 10–15 minutes) 
and on routes serving large numbers of students, especially before and after school. To address these issues, 
DASH deployed larger 40-foot and 60-foot buses on the busiest trips, which required additional municipal 
resources and funding.82

Columbus, OH: In 2015, a fare-free pilot program in Columbus, OH found that transit ridership doubled when 
downtown workers were given access to free bus passes.86 As a result, Columbus’s Downtown C-Pass program 
was initiated in June 2018 to provide fare-free transit access with a goal to improve the local economy and 
citizens’ perspectives on commuting via transit.87 

The program made unlimited free transit access available to employees of companies and businesses located 
in downtown Columbus. C-Pass is funded by the Mid-Ohio Regional Planning Commission (MORPC) and the 
Capital Crossroads Special Improvement District, the first fare-free program of its kind to be partially funded by 
downtown property owners.88

A 2019 survey of program participants (employers and employees) found that: 

• 

• 

68 percent of respondents attributed C-pass as the reason they started using the bus;

93 percent of new transit riders had access to a private vehicle;

•  New riders were economically diverse, with 46 percent annually earning below $75,000, and the remainder 

earning more than $75,000; and

• 

52 percent of companies reported knowing that employees had relinquished parking because they now take 
Central Ohio Transit Agency (COTA) bus service.89 

The survey showed that transit usage during rush hour had increased by 24 percent. These findings highlight 
the environmental benefits of fare-free transit, in particular potential reductions in vehicle usage and carbon 
emissions. For example, within a year, individuals who switched to Columbus public transit were estimated to 
have saved more than 350,000 gallons of fuel and averted 6.8 million pounds of carbon dioxide emissions.90  

Due to its success, beginning in 2021, the program was renewed for an additional five years.91 The program 
initially began with 230 companies and 7,600 employees enrolled. By 2024, the C-Pass program had 370 
companies enrolled and 12,305 employees enrolled.92 In 2024, an estimated 585,000 rides utilized the C-Pass

Orange County, CA: In September 2021, Orange County began a Youth Ride Free program for children ages 
6–18.94 Schools collaborated with the Orange County Transportation Authority (OCTA) in outreach efforts, and 
distributed unique physical passes to families. In cases where a school did not participate, students or guardians 
retained the option to request annual passes directly via OCTA’s website or via mail. 

In late January 2022, OCTA collected 581 community comments on the program, with roughly 96 percent of 
respondents expressing support. The primary reasons cited were general support (39.6 percent), academic 
success (34.8 percent), and financial benefits (27.9 percent).

Within the first 4.5 months of the program, OCTA buses had more than 18,000 youth program participants taking 
over 672,000 rides.95 OCTA found that most of the trips were school-related and that approximately 70 percent of 
participants were high schoolers. The program was initially planned to last only six months. However, due to its 
success, it received additional funding to continue its operations through present day.  

OCTA staff estimated an annual decrease in fixed-route fare revenue of about $2.2 million, and $23,000 for 
paratransit fare revenue, due to the Youth Ride Free program.96 The revenue loss will continue to be subsidized 
by state funding from CalTrans’ Low Carbon Transit Operations Program (LCTOP), which funds transit agencies’ 
efforts to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and improve mobility, particularly for disadvantaged communities.97 

By the end of January 2025, the Youth Ride Free program exceeded 10.6 million rides and had over 55,000 
participants.98 While youth only accounted for 2–3 percent of bus ridership prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, 
youth rose to 10 percent of bus ridership following the fare-free program.99 In April 2025, Orange County 
approved the use of additional funds to continue the Youth Ride Free program through September 2027.100

Lesson: Free transit programs for youth are primarily utilized by older (i.e., high school aged) populations for

RECOMMENDATIONS

Given the strong evidence that fare-free transit increases ridership, reduces household transportation costs, 
improves access to essential services, and helps build lifelong transit habits, this report recommends that 
Hawaiʻi should enact statewide legislation establishing a permanent, year-round fare-free transit program for all 
residents age 18 and under. 

We acknowledge that affordable transit access is important for all people, and ultimately the most effective 
fare free programs (such as the ones provided by the County of Hawaiʻi and in Albuquerque) provide universal 
transit access. However, providing statewide access for youth is an important step to make this vision a reality. 
Moreover, as demonstrated in this report, young people are the largest demographic of individuals who do not 
drive and are currently underserved by our public transit system—particularly on Oʻahu. 

Although there is a patchwork of reduced-fare and limited fare-free programs available in Hawaiʻi—including 
the HiDOE’s EXPRESS program and county-specific initiatives—the state still lacks a unified system that 
guarantees all young people equitable access to transportation regardless of county, school type, or family 
income. A statewide program modeled after Washington’s Youth Ride Free initiative would create consistent 
access, reduce administrative burden, and ensure that county transit agencies are financially supported as they 
expand youth ridership.

KEIKI RIDE FREE • 21

KEY COMPONENTS OF A FREE TRANSIT PROGRAM 

1. Provide statewide, zero-fare transit access for youth (18 and under), as well as caregivers accompanying 
young children.

Based on national best practices, youth should be able to ride any county transit system at no cost, and at any 
time of the year. Making the program year-round—not limited to the school year—ensures that young people can 
reliably travel to summer programs, employment, sports, cultural activities, medical care, and family obligations. 

Because guardians may hesitate to allow younger children to travel alone, Hawaiʻi should include a free caregiver 
pass for an adult accompanying youth riders aged 12 or younger.

2. Simple, pass-free boarding to reduce administrative burden and increase transit access.

The statewide program should allow all youth ages 18 and under to board county buses (and the rail on Oʻahu) 
without needing a transit pass, following the proven Washington State model. Eliminating passes removes 
enrollment barriers, shortens boarding times, and significantly reduces administrative costs associated with 
issuing, replacing, and verifying transit passes. National best practices consistently show that fare-free programs 
experience higher utilization and lower overhead when riders are not required to carry specialized transit passes.

To support appropriate fare enforcement while maintaining barrier-free access, bus drivers could ask youth who 
appear older than 18 to show a student ID or state-issued ID. This light-touch identification approach ensures 
program integrity without placing unnecessary burdens on families, schools, or transit agencies. By relying on 
existing IDs rather than creating new pass systems, counties can focus resources on improving service quality 
rather than managing fare administration.

22 • KEIKI RIDE FREE

3. Create a statewide grant/fund to reimburse county agencies.

To fund the program sustainably, the Hawaiʻi State Legislature should establish a dedicated state funding 
mechanism—similar to Washington’s Climate Commitment Act funding—providing transit agencies recurring 
reimbursements for foregone youth fare revenue. Formula-based allocations could be tied to each county’s 
youth population, ridership levels, and demonstrated need, while encouraging counties to expand safe, reliable 
service. 

Established by the Hawaiʻi State Legislature as Act 96 in 2025, the Hawaiʻi Green Fee could also serve as a 
potential source of funding to support the youth transit program. Reducing vehicle travel emissions would 
fall within the law’s purpose to protect the state’s natural environment and infrastructure from the impacts of 
climate change.

4. Require county transit agencies to document program costs.

To ensure long-term success and legislative oversight, the program should include an annual statewide report 
summarizing:

• 

Total youth ridership and changes over time; 

•  Peak periods and heavily used routes; 

•  Program benefits, such as youth access to education and extracurricular activities, as well as impacts on 

vehicle traffic and congestion near schools; and 

• 

Any operational challenges, such as overcrowding or changes in service needs. 

Survey data should be collected in the first two years of the program and periodically thereafter. Combining 
ridership statistics with qualitative insights from families, schools, and youth will help counties refine service 
and help the Legislature assess long-term impacts and funding needs.
