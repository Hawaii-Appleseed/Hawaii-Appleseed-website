1. RENTAL AGREEMENTS

Written vs. Verbal Leases: §521-22

A verbal agreement creates a month-to-month tenancy and cannot exceed one year. Verbal agreements are valid 
but difficult to enforce. Both parties should always document terms in writing. New or changed terms require 
written agreement by both parties.

Month-to-Month Tenancy: §521-22

The default tenancy type when no fixed term is set. Either party may end it with proper advance notice. Offers 
flexibility but less long-term stability than a fixed lease. Rent or rule changes, as well as termination, all require 
written notice. The legal requirement is 45 days’ notice to end a month-to-month tenancy. Landlords may not 
change terms without notice.

Prohibited Lease Clauses: §521-31 to §521-35

Any clause that strips away a tenant’s legal rights is void even if signed. Tenants cannot waive the right to a 
habitable unit, the right to sue, or the right to proper notice. Landlords cannot include clauses limiting liability for 
negligence, requiring confession of judgment1, or waiving tenant rights. Such clauses are unenforceable by law.

Landlords may charge a screening fee, but only to cover the actual cost of obtaining information about the

Maximum Deposit Amount: §521-44(b)

•  Base deposit: no more than 1 month’s rent. 
•  Pet deposit: An additional pet deposit of up to 1 month’s rent may be charged if a tenant has a pet. 
•  Note: Service and assistance animals are not pets under §515-3 and a pet deposit is not permitted for them.

Landlords may only deduct for:
•  Unpaid rent: 
•  Cleaning if the unit is left dirtier than received;
•  Damage beyond normal wear and tear; and
•  Unreturned keys or access devices. 

Normal wear and tear such as faded paint and carpet wear 
cannot be deducted.

Itemized Deductions: §521-44(c)

Any retained portion must be accompanied by a written itemized list with receipts or invoices. A vague 
“damages” statement is not legally sufficient. A lump-sum deduction without itemization is invalid.

Property Transfer: §521-44(f)

If a building is sold, the new owner is responsible for all existing deposits. The new owner must give written notice 
of deposit amounts within 20 days of transfer. If they fail to do so, the law assumes the deposit equals one month’s 
rent at the tenant’s original rate. A property sale does not affect existing leases. The new owner takes over the 
property bound by all existing rental agreements and their terms.

3. RENT & FEES

When Rent Is Due: §521-21(b)

Rent is due at the beginning of each rental period (typically 
1st day of the month) unless the lease states otherwise. 
Landlords cannot demand rent before it is due. Rent is pro-
rated daily if the tenancy starts mid-period.

Rent Increases: §521-21(d)(e)

•  Month-to-month tenancy: minimum 45 days’ written 

notice before an increase takes effect. 
•  Week-to-week: 15 days’ written notice.

Late Fees: §521-21

Retalitory Rent Increases  <!>

Any rent increase within 60 days of a 
tenant complaint or repair request is 
presumed retaliatory. The burden falls 
on the landlord to prove otherwise 
in court. Retaliation does not require 
a formal complaint. A landlord who 
threatens or imposes a rent increase 
in direct response to a tenant’s 
maintenance request may be in 
violation of §521-74.

Late fees are only enforceable if the lease explicitly allows them. There is no mandatory statewide grace period. If 
the lease is silent on late fees, a landlord generally cannot charge one. Any late fee must be reasonable.

Postdated Check: §521-44(e)

Landlords cannot require tenants to provide postdated checks or negotiable instruments for future rent payments. 
Violation gives tenants grounds for legal remedy.

4. LANDLORD OBLIGATIONS

Habitable Unit: §521-42

Landlords must maintain the unit in compliance with health and building codes at all times. 
•  Required: weatherproofing, working plumbing, hot and cold water, heat, safe electrical, smoke detectors, and 

freedom from pests. 

Tenants should report issues in writing: first to the landlord, and if no action is taken the tenant may report to the 
Department of Health (for health and sanitation violations), the respective County Planning or Housing Department 
(for building code violations), or the Office of Consumer Protection (for general landlord-tenant complaints). 

Reporting issues to the landlord triggers the landlord’s legal duty to act within a reasonable time. Landlords cannot 
charge tenants for repairs that are the landlord’s obligation.

Essential Services: §521-42, §521-57

Landlords cannot shut off water, electricity, gas, or other 
essential services to pressure a tenant out even if rent is owed. 
Shutting off utilities as a form of eviction is unlawful regardless 
of rent status. Only a court order can remove a tenant.

Required Disclosures: §521-43

Illegal Self-Help Eviction <!>

Shutting off utilities to force a tenant out 
is an illegal self-help eviction. Penalty: 
3× monthly rent OR $1,000, whichever is 
greater, plus any other damages.

Before or at lease signing, the landlord must provide in 
writing: the name and address of the property owner, the name and address of any agent authorized to receive 
notices, and a copy of any written lease. Failure to disclose gives the tenant a $100 claim.

5. TENANT OBLIGATIONS

Pay Rent On Time: §521-51

Tenants must pay rent when due. After proper written notice to pay or quit, non-payment can lead to eviction 
proceedings. Landlords cannot charge rent for any period the unit was uninhabitable due to the landlord’s failure 
to maintain habitability.

Maintain the Unit: §521-51, §521-52

Tenants must keep the unit reasonably clean, dispose of trash properly, and avoid causing damage. Normal wear 
and tear minor scuffs, carpet wear, faded paint is not a valid deposit deduction. Actual damage—such as holes in 
walls, broken fixtures, or stained carpet—is a valid deduction.

Subletting: §521-31

If the lease requires landlord consent to sublet, the tenant must obtain it in writing. Subletting without permission 
can be grounds for eviction. By default tenants are free to sublet unless the lease says otherwise. If the lease 
requires landlord approval to sublet, the landlord cannot unreasonably refuse. If the lease prohibits subletting 
outright, that prohibition is enforceable and the tenant cannot sublet.

6. ACCESS & PRIVACY

Required Notice Before Entry: §521-53

Landlords must give at least 2 days’ written notice before entering a unit for inspections, repairs, or showings. 
Entry must occur at reasonable hours (generally 8am–8pm). Tenants may refuse entry if proper notice was not 
given, except in genuine emergencies. Landlords may not abuse entry rights as a form of harassment.

Tenant-Granted Access: §521-53

Tenants may waive the 2-day notice in writing for a specific entry. This waiver applies only to that specific 
occasion and does not eliminate the general notice requirement.

7. ENDING THE TENANCY

Tenant Ends Month-to-Month: §521-71

Tenants must give at least 28 days’ written notice to end a month-to-month tenancy. The lease may require longer 
notice. Tenants may also terminate early if the unit becomes uninhabitable (§521-61). Landlords cannot penalize 
tenants for early termination if the unit was uninhabitable.

Landlord Ends Month-to-Month: §521-71

Landlords must give 45 days’ written notice to end a month-to-month tenancy without cause. If the tenant 
has violated the lease, 28 days’ notice is required after a notice to cure has been given first. Tenants cannot be 
removed without a court order even after the notice period expires.

Fixed-Term Lease: §521-71

A fixed-term lease ends on its expiration date with no additional notice required. If the tenant remains past the 
end date without a new agreement, a month-to-month tenancy is created. Accepting rent from a holdover tenant 
also creates a month-to-month tenancy.

LANDLORD-TENANT CODE • 7

Condo Conversion: §521-38

If a building is converting to condominiums, tenants must receive at least 120 days’ written notice before their 
tenancy is terminated. Tenants do not have a statutory right to purchase their unit in a condo conversion. The 120-
day notice is the only protection provided under §521-38.

Abandonment: §521-70

A unit may be considered abandoned if a tenant is physically absent for 20 or more consecutive days, has not paid 
rent during that period, and has clearly indicated by their actions or words that they do not intend to return. A 
tenant who continues to pay rent during an extended absence, such as a vacation, cannot be considered to have 
abandoned the unit regardless of how long they are away. 

If the lease requires the tenant to notify the landlord of extended absences, failing to do so may make the tenant 
liable for any damage that occurs in their absence.

8. EVICTIONS & LEGAL REMEDIES

Legal Eviction Process: §521-68 to §521-74 

A landlord cannot physically remove a tenant, change locks, 
or remove appliances without a court order. The process must 
follow: written notice → landlord files in court → hearing → 
judge’s order → sheriff enforcement. Self-help evictions are 
illegal and expose landlords to significant damages.

Retaliatory Eviction:  §521-74

Illegal Self-Help Evictions 
Include <!>

Changing locks, removing doors 
or windows, shutting off utilities, 
removing appliances, or threatening 
or intimidating the tenant to leave.

It is illegal to evict a tenant or raise rent in retaliation for: 
reporting code violations, joining a tenants’ union, or 
requesting legally required repairs. Any eviction or rent increase within 60 days of a protected action is legally 
presumed to be retaliatory. The burden of proof lies with the landlord to prove otherwise in court.

Repair & Deduct Remedy: §521-64, §521-65

If a landlord fails to repair after written notice AND a 
government agency has cited a health or safety violation, the 
tenant may—after 1 week of landlord inaction—repair and 
deduct the cost from rent. Deduction is limited to 1 month’s 
rent per repair, with receipts required.

Rent Withholding: §521-78

If the unit is legally uninhabitable due to the landlord’s failure, 
the tenant may be entitled to withhold rent or terminate after 
proper written notice. Tenants should consult the Landlord-
Tenant Information Center or an attorney before withholding 
rent. Failure to follow the legal process can result in eviction.

Small claims court is available for disputes up to $10,000, or for security deposit disputes of any amount. Neither

When a tenant does not pay rent, the landlord must serve

GAPS IN THE CURRENT LANDLORD 
TENANT CODE

HRS Chapter 521 provides a meaningful framework for landlord-tenant protections. However there are serious 
structural gaps that undermine housing justice in the rental markets. Most glaring, the nature of enforcement falls 
to the party whose rights have been violated. 

This means a landlord or tenant must independently: 
•  Know what rights are granted under the landlord tenant code;
•  Know how to exercise those rights and communicate them effectively to the other party;
•  Know what agency or department to log a complaint with; and
•  Know how to file a claim in court to recover damages or losses.
• 
One of the most significant gaps in the landlord tenant code is that it acts largely as a self-help law. Both landlords 

10 • LANDLORD-TENANT CODE

and tenants must independently identify, assert, and litigate their own protections. This structural gap falls 
hardest on tenants. 

Nationally less than 5 percent of tenants have an attorney in eviction court, compared to more than 80 percent 
of landlords.2 Eviction proceedings are complex and intimidating processes in which unrepresented tenants face 
a significant structural disadvantage. Many tenants lose their homes not because they lack a valid defense, but 
because they lack the resources to use it. As a result, case outcomes often reflect disparities in representation 
rather than the underlying merits of the case.3

Compounding this, the no-cause termination loophole allows landlords to end a month-to-month tenancy for any 
reason, or no reason at all, with 45 days’ written notice. Although §521-74 prohibits explicit retaliatory eviction, 
landlords can sidestep this protection entirely by simply issuing a 45-day no-cause notice rather than citing a 
reason. This makes the anti-retaliation provision largely unenforceable in practice. 

Just Cause Eviction would require a landlord to have a legally recognized reason before terminating a tenancy, 
defining two categories of permissible grounds: at-fault causes based on tenant behavior such as nonpayment of 
rent, lease violations, or property damage; and no-fault causes based on legitimate landlord decisions unrelated 
to tenant conduct, such as owner move-in or major renovation.

The Furman Center’s analysis of good cause eviction acknowledges that just cause protections provide real 
benefits in promoting housing stability and reducing evictions, which are associated with poor health outcomes, 
future housing instability, and decreased earning.4 Longitudinal studies on health outcomes and housing 
instability and eviction have been linked to the initiation of drug use, elevated risk of blood pressure and weight 
gain, higher risk of heart disease, and increased risk of domestic abuse that impact both individual adults and 
families with children.5 

At the same time, the Furman Center notes that poorly designed just cause requirements risk discouraging future 
housing projects, increasing eviction costs that get passed on to all tenants, and narrowing the choices available 
to renters in the market.6 These are nuanced and legitimate policy design considerations, not arguments against 
just cause itself, and point toward the importance of thoughtful policy crafting and implementation, including 
appropriate exemptions for new construction and small owner-occupied properties. 

Even with those guardrails in place, just cause addresses the terms of displacement, not the conditions that 
make displacement so devastating in the first place.The deeper question is what just cause eviction can actually 
accomplish in a market where tenants have nowhere else to go.

WHAT REFORM LOOKS LIKE

Just Cause Eviction (JCE) laws establish minimum legal standards that landlords must meet before terminating a 
tenancy. Rather than replacing HRS Chapter 521, JCE reforms would plug the specific gaps identified in Part II. 

The framework presented in Table 1 incorporates lessons from jurisdictions that have enacted just cause 
protections and reflects the design considerations identified by the NYU Furman Center, whose analysis cautions 
that poorly crafted just cause requirements risk discouraging future housing production, increasing costs passed 
on to tenants, and straining court resources.

ELIMINATING NO-CAUSE TERMINATION

As established in Part II of this policy brief, the self help-nature of the law is the most significant structural gap in 
Hawaiʻi’s landlord-tenant law, and the no-cause termination loophole compounds it directly. When a landlord can 
end a tenancy without stating a reason, a tenant’s only recourse is to independently identify, assert, and mitigate 
a retaliation claim they may not know they have, against a landlord who was never required to give them a reason 
to begin with.

Eliminating no-cause termination is not a standalone fix, but a reform that makes the rest of the framework’s 
protections enforceable. All eviction notices should cite a legally recognized cause, at-fault or no-fault, supported 
by documentation. Eviction notices must prohibit false, pretextural, or discriminatory termination. The burden 
is on the landlord to verify their stated reason or compensate the tenant with one month’s rent plus the security 
deposit required for their new unit.

CONCLUSION

Just Cause Eviction, however well designed, addresses only one dimension of the power imbalance between 
landlords and tenants. The deeper structural problem is supply. 

When housing production consistently fails to meet demand, as it has for decades in Hawaiʻi, landlords hold all 
the leverage.7 In a market where only about 4 percent of units are vacant and available8 and well over half of 
renters are cost-burdened,9 landlords can raise rents and tighten screening with relatively little risk of prolonged 
vacancy. Under these conditions, tenant protections provide a legal floor but cannot provide tenant power. A 
tenant who is protected from arbitrary eviction but has nowhere else to go is still a tenant without a real choice. 

Genuine tenant leverage requires a market where landlords compete for tenants: where the threat of a vacant unit 
carries financial loss, and where a tenant who leaves for better conditions, fairer treatment, or a more favorable 
location can find one.10 That kind of leverage does not come from regulation alone. It comes from sufficient supply 
that tenants have options. 

Delaying or blocking the multifamily housing that Hawaiʻi’s rental market needs does not protect tenants. Rather, 
doing so only maintains the conditions that give landlords outsized power over them.11 Just Cause Eviction is a 
necessary protection but it is not a substitute for the supply-side reforms that would shift the underlying balance 
of power in Hawaiʻi’s rental market toward the people who need housing most.
