OVER THE PAST SEVERAL DECADES, the United States has experienced a dramatic rise in the frequency, severity, 
and geographic reach of climate-driven disasters—from hurricanes and floods, to wildfires and extreme heat.

These tragic events devastate communities and take lives. They also strain the financial infrastructure that 
underpins recovery: the property insurance system.

As disaster claims mount, private insurers are taking in record profits while adopting strategies that harm 
consumers. These tactics include pleading depletion of capital reserves despite record CEO pay,1 retreating from 
high-risk markets, or sharply increasing premiums. 

In a self-reinforcing cycle, these firms then reinvest their profits into the very fossil fuel companies that intensify the 
climate disasters that force them to abandon vulnerable markets.2

In the wake of this abandonment, federal and state governments have been forced to intervene with a patchwork

of strategies, ranging from legislative reforms and public “insurers of last resort,” to expansion of programs like 
the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP). 

At the same time, legal doctrines such as subrogation, shifting norms in public-private responsibility, and the 
growing role of catastrophe modeling are redefining how insurance works in the age of accelerating climate change. 

These pressures are more acute in Hawaiʻi: over 60 percent of our housing stock was constructed before 1990. This 
aging housing stock carries added risk, and requires regular maintenance and costly resilience upgrades. These 
are major financial barriers for many in a state with some of the highest housing costs in the U.S.3 

Additionally, 40 percent of Hawaiʻi’s available housing stock is in multifamily buildings, structures that require 
property insurance not just as a financial safeguard, but as a precondition for mobility, homeownership and 
equity. Without insurance, rents increase, mortgages collapse, units become unsellable, and entire condominium 
associations can be left exposed to catastrophic risk.

Two major sets of factors interact to drive instability in Hawaiʻi’s insurance sector: 

•  Property-specific issues, such as building age, deferred maintenance and loss history; and 

• 

Systemic dynamics, such as global reinsurance markets, catastrophe risk and regulatory constraints. 

Between 2018 and 2023, nonrenewals for insurance policies increased by nearly 216 percent across the state.4 
Between 2021 to 2024, Hawaiʻi homeowners saw an average increase in insurance premiums of 12 percent,5 and 
condo Home Owners Associations have seen an average increase of 16 percent, with fee increases ranging from $1 
to more than $2,000.6

This report investigates how climate change is reshaping the structure, function and equity of insurance markets—
nationally and in Hawaiʻi. It explores:

• 

• 

• 

• 

The historical interaction between climate disasters, market retrenchment and government intervention;

The particular vulnerabilities facing Hawaiʻi’s housing insurance system;

The legal, regulatory, and market tools being mobilized to manage escalating risk; and

The equity and governance implications of an insurance market in retreat.

By analyzing both past disasters and unfolding crises, the report captures a system in flux—where each disaster 
reshapes not only the built environment but also the financial rules, legal responsibilities, and social norms that 
determine who is protected, who pays for recovery, and who is left behind. 

To build a more resilient and equitable future for Hawaiʻi’s residents, this report recommends: 

1.  Enabling the state to subrogate companies most responsible for climate disasters; 

2.  Reshaping land use patterns to create natural resiliency buffers; 

3.  Retrofitting older housing stock to withstand climate impacts; 

4.  Adopting and enforcing updated building codes for new construction; and 

5.  Expanding education on risk management and available resources to help households and communities take 

preventative measures.

GLOSSARY OF TERMS

•  Catastrophic risk: Low-frequency, high-severity risk that can cause large, simultaneous losses across 
many policyholders, often overwhelming the financial capacity of insurers. Catastrophic risks—such 
as major hurricanes, wildfires, earthquakes, or other climate-driven disasters—are difficult to diversify 
and often require reinsurance, public backstops, or government intervention to manage.

• 

• 

• 

Insurability: The extent to which a risk can be covered by an insurer at a reasonable price and under 
workable conditions. A risk is considered insurable when losses are predictable enough to price, 
not excessively likely or severe, and can be spread across a broad pool. As climate-driven disasters 
become more frequent and severe, some areas and properties may become less insurable, even if 
insurance products still technically exist.

Insurance: A financial arrangement in which individuals or organizations transfer risk to an insurer 
in exchange for paying premiums. Insurance works by pooling risks across many policyholders, 
allowing the costs of losses to be shared.

Insurer of last resort: A government-run or government-backed insurance entity that provides 
coverage where private insurers will not, typically because the risk is considered too high, 
unpredictable, or unprofitable. These programs act as a safety net to ensure that people and 
communities can still obtain essential insurance in areas facing catastrophic or concentrated risks, 
such as hurricanes, wildfires, floods, or volcanic eruptions. 

•  Moral hazard: A situation in which individuals or organizations change their behavior because they 
are insulated from the consequences of risk, often due to insurance coverage or public support. In 
climate-related disasters, moral hazard may occur when properties continue to be built or rebuilt in 
high-risk areas because insurance, subsidies, or public aid reduce the perceived cost of exposure.

•  Prior approval (insurance rate regulation): A state regulatory process that requires insurance 
companies to submit proposed rates for review and obtain approval from the state insurance 
regulator before the rates can be used. Prior approval is designed to ensure that rates are not 
excessive, inadequate, or unfairly discriminatory, especially in markets affected by rising climate-
related risks. 

•  Reinsurance: Insurance that insurance companies purchase to protect themselves against very large 

or catastrophic losses.

•  Subrogation: The legal process that allows an insurer to seek reimbursement from a third party that 
caused the loss after the insurer has paid a claim to the policyholder. Subrogation shifts costs back to 
the party responsible for the damage, helping to reduce overall losses in the insurance system.

ISSUE OVERVIEW

Property insurance is not just a financial safeguard—it also functions as a gatekeeper to the U.S. housing system. 
Access to coverage determines access to mortgage finance and, without that, homes become uninsurable, 
unsellable and ultimately inaccessible. This dynamic is especially acute in multifamily housing markets such as 
Hawaiʻi, where approximately 40 percent of the existing housing stock is multifamily.7

Nationally, climate change is destabilizing insurance markets through cascading effects on affordability and 
availability. Between 2020 and 2023, average homeowners insurance premiums rose by about 30 percent.8 At the 
same time, the costs of home repair climbed by more than 50 percent in just two years, further amplifying insurer 
exposure.9 These pressures have led private insurers to retreat from an increasing number of high-risk markets, 
leaving entire regions with limited or no access to new coverage.10

In California, the top three insurers stopped writing new homeowners policies in wildfire-prone zones.11 In 2023 
alone, State Farm declined to renew over 70,000 policies.12 

Florida’s state-backed insurer of last resort, Citizens Property Insurance Corporation, has ballooned to more 
than 1.4 million policies, making it one of the 10 largest insurers in the nation.13 Such programs are designed as 
temporary backstops, but Florida’s program now shoulders systemic levels of risk.

The problem extends to all forms and tenure of housing. After the 2021 Surfside condominium collapse in Miami, 
Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac imposed stricter financing standards on multifamily properties across the nation.14 
Thousands of condominium units across the United States were placed on “blacklists,” making them ineligible for 
federally backed mortgages (Figure 1).15 

Deferred maintenance and underfunded reserves in older buildings have pushed many associations into an 
uninsurable category, with policies canceled or premiums spiking by multiples.16

The Urban Climate Change Research Network has described this convergence of climate pressures, market 
withdrawals, and structural vulnerabilities as a “rising tide of risk.” Without proactive intervention, that tide 
threatens to make housing financially inaccessible to tens of millions of people, undermining not only property 
markets but the broader social contract around homeownership of economic stability.17 

In Hawaiʻi, these risks are destabilizing the property insurance market, particularly for condominiums. Between 
2018 and 2023, nonrenewals increased by 216 percent statewide.18 Premiums have risen in tandem, threatening 
the financial viability of condominium associations and pushing households toward financial insecurity. 

Because insurance is a prerequisite for mortgage lending, this instability threatens not only individual households 
but also the broader housing market. Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell recently underscored this risk, noting 
that insurers and banks are “pulling out of coastal areas, areas where there are a lot of fires,” and predicted that in 
“10 to 15 years there are going to be regions of the country where you can’t get a mortgage.”19

History demonstrates that when private insurers retreat—as they did in the wake of Hurricane ʻIniki, Hurricane 
Katrina, or the California wildfires—the burden shifts to taxpayers, homeowners, and state-backed insurers of last 
resort.

Hawaiʻi is one of the most unaffordable markets for both renters and homeowners. Residents already feel boxed 
out of ever-rising housing prices. Barriers are particularly steep for would-be first time buyers.20 The median cost 
to own for residents has increased dramatically in the last five years for both single family homes and condos, 
while the share of residents who could afford it has dropped (Figure 2).21 

Rising insurance premiums and questions of uninsurability compound the problem. Homes saddled with 
increasing HOA fees demand a higher monthly payment on top of the mortgage. Where properties cannot be 
insured, only cash buyers can compete. Few local residents have $600,000–$950,000 in cash on hand to compete 
with out-of-state and international buyers. First time homebuyers are also increasingly being locked out of 
generally more affordable older condo stock.22

Climate Change

The accelerating pace of climate-related disasters is the 
most powerful force destabilizing insurance markets. 
Hurricanes, wildfires and floods are now more frequent and 
more destructive than when these markets were created.23 
To keep pace with insured losses and the increased chance 
of having to pay out potential losses, insurance companies 
must increase capital reserves or purchase additional 
reinsurance—a cost directly passed to the consumer.24  

Each new climate disaster carries the risk of multi-billion 
dollar claims, forcing insurers to hold even higher capital 
reserves and purchase costly reinsurance–or withdraw 
entirely from areas deemed too risky. These expenses cascade 
directly to consumers in the form of rapidly rising premiums.

Insurer Market Exit

As catastrophic risk grows, insurers are pulling out of 
vulnerable markets.25 In Hawaiʻi, insurer market exit 
increased premiums for condos, while some 400 condos 
in Hawaiʻi don’t have 100 percent coverage. An increasing 
number of single family homes are also facing non-renewal 
due to risks associated with single-wall construction.26 27 
These market exits create “insurance voids,” where 
properties can no longer secure mortgages, saleability 
lowers as homes spend more time on the market, and condo 
associations face financial distress.

Deferred Maintenance & HOAs

Deferred maintenance—particularly in Hawaiʻi’s multifamily buildings—has 
become a critical issue limiting residents’ ability to buy and sell older units that 
are often the most attainable and naturally affordable options in the market.28 29 

The high risk factor for deferred maintenance in Hawaiʻi housing is due 
largely to the fact that the majority of our existing housing stock is built 
before 1990.30 Standards of construction and insurance have changed over 
time as building codes are updated often in response to natural disasters. 
The typical single-wall construction of the mid-20th century requires 
more preventative measures,31 while older condos where boards have not 
raised maintenance fees in decades have less money to address routine 
maintenance.32 When these units are deemed uninsurable, it makes it more 
onerous for residents to finance. 

Regulatory Pressures

State-level regulation also shapes market outcomes. In Florida and California, 
laws that cap or slow premium increases are designed to protect consumers 
but often discourage insurers from staying in the market.33 “Prior approval” 
systems, where regulators must authorize any rate changes, protect consumers 
but may also limit an insurer’s flexibility to respond to new risk data.34 

Increasingly, climate change is driving higher and more volatile losses—costs 
that insurers must carry and ultimately seek to recover.35 Regulators play a 
critical role in balancing consumer protection with market stability, ensuring 
coverage remains accessible without imposing restrictions that risk driving 
insurers out. The central question is where, and how, those rising costs 
should be recouped.

Moral Hazard and Risk Modeling

Finally, insurers are increasingly using advanced catastrophe models to set 
rates at the property level. These models reward proactive risk reduction 
measures—such as roof reinforcements, storm shutters, or wildfire 
hardening—with lower premiums. In theory, this reduces moral hazard by 
aligning cost with risk. In practice, many households and associations cannot 
afford expensive upgrades, leaving them exposed to higher rates or outright 
denial of coverage.36 While less immediately destabilizing than climate 
shocks or market exits, this shift is reshaping insurability and limiting the 
accessibility of housing.

EQUITY AND CLIMATE 
JUSTICE

The sharpest equity impact of insurance market instability is the surge in premiums and non-renewals. For many 
households, especially those with limited incomes, dramatic increases can be devastating.

In Hawaiʻi, condo associations have seen an average 16 percent increase in HOA fees since the Lahaina wildfires, 
with some associations reporting overall increases of 1000 percent.37 While wealthier owners may be able to 
absorb higher costs or self-insure, working families often cannot. In practice, this means that the very people most 
in need of financial protection are being pushed out of the insurance market altogether.

When a condominium association is denied coverage, federally-backed lenders such as Fannie Mae and Freddie 
Mac will not approve loans for its units.38 The result is that homes in those buildings lose value, leaving owners 
unable to sell or refinance. For households whose primary wealth is tied to their home, this dynamic represents 
not only rising costs but also a collapse of long-term financial security.

Renters are also affected, though their exposure is less visible. In multifamily housing, insurance premiums 
are passed on through maintenance fees or rents. As associations face higher costs, landlords and unit owners 
adjust rents upward to compensate. At the same time, some associations or property owners respond to 
mounting premiums by deferring maintenance. This creates a double burden for tenants: higher rents paired with 
deteriorating housing quality.

Over time, the layering of these pressures deepens existing wealth gaps. Low-income households, renters and 
Native Hawaiian families with limited intergenerational wealth are disproportionately exposed. As insurability 
declines, lenders become wary and developers disinvest, compounding cycles of housing insecurity and 
neighborhood decline.39 What begins as a financial recalibration in the insurance sector ultimately produces social 
and spatial inequities across Hawaiʻi. 

In the aftermath of the 2023 Lahaina wildfires a recent survey analyzed by the University of Hawaiʻi Economic 
Research Organization (UHERO), has estimated that approximately 500 people departed Maui as a result of the 
fires (Figure 3). Half of these survey respondents reside in temporary housing, more than a third are currently 
unhoused, living with family or friends, or temporary units without assistance, indicating that displacement 
remains a significant risk.40 Without sufficient affordable housing in Maui or across the state residents are forced 
out or at risk of homelessness.41 Insurance payouts benefit homeowners, leaving the renters of Lahaina at 
disproportional risk of permanent displacement.

CASE STUDIES

Case studies provide a critical window into how legislatures respond when natural disasters threaten the viability 
of home insurance markets. Each event forces policymakers to confront the same dilemma: how to stabilize 
insurance availability without undermining affordability or driving private carriers out of the market. 

By examining the tools deployed in different contexts—from the creation of state-run insurers of last resort, to 
reforms in catastrophe modeling, to federal interventions through programs like the National Flood Insurance 
Plan (NFIP)—we can trace how regulatory choices shape long-term outcomes for homeowners. Looking at these 
responses side by side not only reveals the diversity of strategies, but also highlights their lasting implications for 
equity, access to credit, and the overall resilience of housing systems.

1. FLORIDA: HURRICANE ANDREW & THE EMERGENCE OF STATE INSURANCE

When Hurricane Andrew struck South Florida in August 1992, it left behind one of the costliest natural disasters 
in U.S. history. Winds exceeding 165 miles per hour leveled more than 25,000 homes, damaged over 100,00 more, 
and shuttered 82,000 businesses.46 

In total, the 1992 storm inflicted an estimated $15 billion in insured losses, overwhelming not only residents and 
local economies, but also the state’s insurance sector.47 Adjusted for inflation, that’s over $34 billion in 2025 dollars. 

Prior to landfall, industry experts estimated a worst-case insured loss of around $4–5 billion.48 When the actual 
loss was around three times more, eight insurers became insolvent in Andrew’s wake. Florida homeowners, 
already reeling from physical destruction, were suddenly burdened with skyrocketing insurance costs.

The collapse of the private insurance market prompted the state to take unprecedented action. Lawmakers first 
created two temporary insurance pools: 

• 

• 

The Florida Windstorm Underwriting Association (FWUA), which offered limited coverage in coastal areas; and 

The Florida Residential Property and Casualty Joint Underwriting Association (JUA), which provided broader 
coverage for households unable to secure private policies.49  

In 2002, these entities were merged to form Citizens Property Insurance Corporation (CPIC), a nonprofit, tax-exempt 
public insurer designed to serve as the state’s insurer of last resort. CPIC quickly grew to become one of the largest 
property insurers in Florida, filling the gap left by private carriers that refused to re-enter high-risk markets.

To stabilize the market, Florida also introduced a moratorium on insurer withdrawals, preventing companies from 
exiting too quickly and leaving residents stranded.50 To improve future resilience, the state created the Florida 
Commission on Hurricane Loss Projection Methodology (FCHLPM), tasked with setting standards for catastrophe 
modeling and probable maximum loss calculations. 

These regulatory innovations—particularly the development of CPIC and the creation of rigorous catastrophe 
modeling standards—marks a significant shift in regulatory experimentation in the aftermath of repeated disasters.

At the same time, Florida’s legal reforms aimed at curbing insurance litigation have drawn criticism. Lawsuits by 
policy holders whose claims were denied remain among the highest in the nation; premiums continue to rank near 
the top nationally; and consumer advocates argue that recent tort reforms have largely benefited insurers more 
than residents.51 

Reporting has also highlighted concerts with CPIC’s use of administrative hearings. Thousands of cases have been 
delivered to the Division of Administrative Hearings (DOAH), where critics argue that homeowners face procedural 
disadvantages. CPIC prevails more than 90 percent of the time in these hearings.52

Hurricane Andrew was a turning point in the relationship between climate disasters and the insurance 
industry. It exposed the limits of private capital to absorb catastrophic risk and demonstrated the necessity of 
state intervention. 

The legacy of Andrew continues to shape Florida’s insurance system today, where CPIC remains a dominant player. 
However, Florida consumers continue to face some of the highest premiums in the nation, weaker protections in 
disputes, and an influx of undercapitalized carriers that may not withstand the disaster.

2. HAWAIʻI: HURRICANE ʻINIKI & THE HAWAIʻI HURRICANE RELIEF FUND

On September 11, 1992, Hurricane ʻIniki struck Kauaʻi as the most powerful hurricane in Hawaiʻi’s recorded 
history.53 With sustained winds of 145 miles per hour, the storm damaged or destroyed more than 14,000 homes 
and left much of the island without power or potable water for weeks.54

Insured losses totaled around $1.6 billion. For insurers, the scale of claims was overwhelming. Many companies 
lacked sufficient reserves to cover payouts, forcing them to withdraw from the market or raise premiums by 
four to five times their previous levels.55 Thousands of households that had just endured catastrophic damage 
suddenly found themselves unable to secure or afford property insurance.

The market collapse pushed Hawaiʻi lawmakers to act. In 1993, the state created the Hawaiʻi Hurricane Relief Fund 
(HHRF), a public program designed to provide hurricane coverage when private insurers refused.56 At its peak, 
the HHRF served over 155,000 policyholders statewide,57 stabilizing access to coverage and preventing a broader 
mortgage crisis. Once the private market began to recover, the HHRF was wound down and ceased writing 
policies in 2000.58 But the memory of ʻIniki lingered. When insurers again began retreating in 2024, lawmakers 
revived the HHRF through Act 296 (2025),59 and expanded its authority to cover condominium and townhouse 
associations (AOAOs) denied by at least two private insurers.60

HHRF was the second insurer of last resort. The first—Hawaii Property Insurance Association (HPIA)—was 
established in 1991 and is still active today. The HPIA is the only insurer across the nation that covers lava damage 
in the highest-risk zones, Lava Zone 1 & 2.61

The long-term lesson from ʻIniki is twofold. First, even in a relatively small and geographically isolated market 
like Hawaiʻi, insurers can retreat rapidly when faced with catastrophic loss, leaving entire communities stranded. 
Second, public insurance mechanisms can buy time and maintain market stability, but their durability depends on 
how they are financed. 

Under Act 296, the HHRF carries about $170 million in reserves and insures up to $90 million per building, with the

In an event of that scale, in which the HHRF could not cover the losses, an assessment would be made on private 
insurers to cover the loss. That assessment would be passed on to policyholders across the state and increase 
premiums on homeowners and auto insurance for everyone.71 This exposure—that a large-scale climate-driven 
disaster would drive up insurance premiusm for everyone—underscores the necessity of public intervention to fill-
in where private insurance finds the risk to be too high.

3. LOUISIANA: HURRICANE KATRINA, STATE & FEDERAL COORDINATION GAPS

When Hurricane Katrina made landfall in August 2005, it 
became the most expensive natural disaster in U.S. history, 
surpassing the damage of Hurricane Andrew.72 The storm 
surge overwhelmed levees and left 80 percent of New Orleans 
underwater. More than 300,000 housing units were damaged 
or destroyed, and insured losses exceeded $40 billion.73 74

However, the majority of flood damage was not covered 
under standard homeowners’ policies, leaving families 
reliant on the National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP). 
That program, already strained, was forced to borrow over 
$20 billion from the U.S. Treasury to meet claims. Even then, 
payouts were slow and uneven.75

For residents, the insurance crisis was compounded by poor 
management and oversight. Many claims were delayed for 
months as FEMA and private insurers struggled to access

The lack of timely insurance payouts by NFIP and FEMA

Katrina made clear that without coordinated federal and 
state reform, insurance markets in disaster-prone regions 
risk not only financial collapse but also deepening cycles of 
displacement and inequality among the people the markets 
are meant to serve. Coverage gaps between private insurance

and inequities in claims handling exacerbated human 
suffering and slowed recovery.

4. CALIFORNIA: PARADISE & PACIFIC PALISADES

The 2018 Camp Fire destroyed more than 18,000 structures in the town of Paradise. At the time, it was the 
deadliest and most destructive wildfire in California state history. The fire killed 85 people, destroyed more than 
18,000 buildings, and spanned almost 240 square miles.81 Some 50,000 people were evacuated and the community 
was left fractured after tens of thousands of people relocated beyond Paradise, and beyond California.82

The event was a glaring reality check. Climate change was indeed making wildfires more frequent, more intense, 
and more costly.83 Losses from the fire overwhelmed insurers. Faced with escalating exposure, major carriers 
such as State Farm began pulling back from the riskiest areas of the California market. Others started raising 
rates and tightening underwriting in wildfire-prone regions. Some chose not to renew policies, or withdrew from 
the market entirely. This made rebuilding nearly impossible for many Paradise residents.84

California’s fallback mechanism, the Fair Access to Insurance Requirements (FAIR) Plan, was originally created in 
1968 as an insurer of last resort.85 In recent years, it has been forced to absorb massive growth in policies as private 
insurers retreated.86 The FAIR Plan is expected to pay out over $4 billion in claims related to the 2025 wildfires. 

The FAIR plan’s coverage limit for residential housing is $3 million, and applies to single-family homes, condos 
and most personal residence policies.87 The plan has also expanded coverage to commercial properties, 
increasing its risk exposure and the state’s financial responsibility in the event of a disaster.88 

By 2025, new fires in high-value urban areas like Pacific Palisades showed that affluent communities were 
not immune from raging climate change-fueled fires, or from financial risk of being unable to recover. The 
destruction of Pacific Palisades, layered on top of the previous catastrophic losses at Paradise, reinforced both 
the limits of private insurers and the vulnerability of the safety net FAIR plan.89

The rapid growth in California’s FAIR Plan coverage shows how climate change is overwhelming the private 
market’s ability to manage catastrophic risk. However, the crisis is not just about climate change outpacing 
insurance; it’s also about insurers fueling the very problem they now refuse to cover. 

Major carriers have profited for decades by investing premiums into fossil fuel companies and underwriting oil 
and gas expansions, while withdrawing coverage from the very homeowners now living with the consequences.95 
The result is a system where insurers protect their balance sheets, public insurers of last resort absorb systemic 
risks they weren’t designed for, and the public is left footing the bill. 

The question is not just how to stabilize the insurance market, but whether regulators will confront the corrosive 
cycle between polluters and insurance profits, and seize opportunities to recoup costs from those responsible, 
rather than saddling the bill at the feet of those most impacted.96 97

Insurance markets are global in nature, shaped by reinsurance flows and actuarial models far beyond Hawaiʻi’s

ACCOUNTABILITY: POLLUTERS MUST PAY

“THE QUESTION REMAINS: WHO PAYS WHEN CATASTROPHIC LOSSES OVERWHELM THE 
STATE-BACKED SYSTEM? IF RESERVES ARE DEPLETED AFTER A MAJOR HURRICANE OR 
TSUNAMI, IT IS TAXPAYERS THAT WILL BE LEFT WITH THE BILL.” 

Fossil fuel combustion has long been known to have adverse effects on our climate. As far back as 1978, the 
planning departments of fossil fuel producers were reporting about long term negative effects to our atmosphere 
from continued extraction and combustion of fossil fuels.98 

Multiple fossil fuel companies had this in mind and predicted changes in a 1988 report: “Sea level, ocean currents, 

22 • CLIMATE DISASTERS

precipitation patterns, regional temperature and weather… these changes could be larger than any that have 
occurred over the last 12,000 years.”99 

Fossil fuel companies have reaped massive financial gains while knowingly expediting climate instability. The 
rest of the world has been forced to deal with the ever intensifying consequences of climate disasters: increased 
likelihoods of stronger hurricanes, dramatic coastal flooding, longer droughts, and more destructive wildfires. The 
costs to insurers for these risks was $140 billion in 2024.100

The Hawaiʻi State Legislature acted decisively in 2024 to reactivate the HHRF, stabilizing coverage for condo 
associations and strengthening the HPIA as the insurer of last resort beyond just lava hazards. However, the 
question remains: Who pays when catastrophic losses overwhelm the state-backed system? If reserves are 
depleted after a major hurricane or wildfire, it is taxpayers that will be left with the bill. 

A more equitable sollution is to authorize the state to act on behalf of insurers like HHRF and HPIA to pursue 
recovery from fossil fuel companies whose products and deception have directly fueled climate-related costs.101 
This would ensure that when disasters drive premiums to unaffordable levels or overwhelm state-backed reserves, 
the costs are not pushed onto Hawaiʻi residents who already face some of the highest housing costs in the country. 

Instead, the state could exercise subrogation on behalf of insurers like HHRF and HPIA, stepping into the shoes 
of policyholders to recover damages directly from fossil fuel companies. Subrogation would ensure that those 
who knowingly exacerbated climate-related insurance  risk can be held financially accountable. This is not only a 
matter of fiscal prudence and protecting the state in the event of a disaster, but also of fairness. Polluters should 
pay for the damages they caused, rather than leaving Hawaiʻi residents to absorb the cost of climate change. 

Other states have already taken action to recoup costs from fossil fuel companies:

•  Vermont: Climate Superfund Act. In 2024, Vermont passed a Climate Superfund Act, holding large fossil fuel 

polluters strictly liable for the damages their emissions have caused since 1995. The law requires companies to 
reimburse the state for climate disaster costs, including losses from catastrophic flooding.102  

This followed a lawsuit in 2021 in which the State of Vermont successfully sued a handful of major fossil fuel 
companies under the Vermont Consumer Protection Act. The state argued that these companies lied about the 
effects that their business would have on global climate. The lawsuit is currently being challenged.

•  New York: Climate Change Superfund Act. In 2024, New York also enacted a “Climate Superfund” law, 

requiring major fossil fuel companies to pay billions of dollars annually into a state fund for climate adaptation 
and disaster costs. The law shifts financial responsibility for damages like flooding and extreme heat from 
taxpayers to those companies that contributed most to climate change.103 This law is also being challenged.

•  California: Lawsuits against ExxonMobil, Shell and Chevron. In 2023, California sued major oil companies, 
including ExxonMobil, Shell and Chevron. The state is seeking billions in damages for decades of deception 
and subsequent harm caused to its taxpayers through climate catastrophes such as wildfires, extreme 
heat and drought. California’s lawsuit also seeks punitive damages and injunctive relief to prevent further 
misleading conduct by polluters.104

•  Hawaiʻi: City & County of Honolulu Fossil Fuel Litigation. In 2019, the Honolulu City Council authorized the 
Honolulu Department of Corporation Counsel to initiate legal action against fossil fuel companies to recoup 
costs that the county will incur to pay for climate adaptation projects and extreme weather impacts. This 
action seeks to recover damages from the fossil fuel companies which profit from oil sales in Hawaiʻi, while 
participating in a decades-long coordinated effort to hide the negative effects of burning fossil fuels to the 
global climate.105

Land use planning sits at the center of long-term disaster resilience. Climate risk is not evenly distributed across 
Hawaiʻi’s islands. Current development patterns have allowed for housing to be built in some of our most 
vulnerable zones: low-lying coastal areas threatened by sea level rise and flooding, or fire-prone landscapes at 
the urban-wildland boundary.106 Continuing to approve the same land use patterns in these areas compounds 
exposure and ultimately shifts escalating insurance and recovery costs on to households and the public.107

Other states are proactively confronting climate risk in land use planning by establishing new policies that directly 
link zoning, infrastructure and hazard reduction. South Carolina mandates a “resiliency element" in all local 
comprehensive plans, so cities and counties must systematically evaluate and address vulnerabilities to flooding, 
sea level rise, and other hazards.108

Similarly, New Jersey and Washington state have made climate hazard assessments a requirement for local 
planning, tying them directly to decisions about new development, infrastructure investments, and zoning 
changes to minimize future disaster exposure.109 Even the federal NFIP incentivizes smarter land use by rewarding 
jurisdictions that adopt floodplain management and mitigation strategies with lower insurance premiums for local 
residents through a Community Rating System (CRS).110

This concept isn’t foreign to Hawaiʻi. After a tsunami devastated Hilo town in 1946 and killed 159 people, the 
community rebuilt on the same spot only to suffer the same devastating consequences in 1960 after another 
tsunami struck.111 

The response to the 1960 tsunami was dramatically different. Hawaiʻi County undertook major changes to land 
use, hazard planning, and disaster resilience to protect the vulnerable coastal city.112 The county government 
mandated a broader oceanside greenbelt bufferzone of lagoons, gardens, and recreational facilities designed to 
protect inland areas by absorbing the impacts of future tsunamis.113

Resilient land use planning gives local governments a powerful tool to contain risk, reduce potential of moral 
hazards, and promote both safety and equity.114 By strengthening zoning and hazard mitigation policies, Hawaiʻi 
can help stabilize insurance markets, relieve low- and moderate-income households of hidden financial burdens, 
and turn smart land use into a foundation for long-term disaster resilience.

NEW HOUSING: BUILT FOR RESILIENCE

Over 60 percent of Hawaiʻi’s housing stock was built before 1990. That means the majority of our housing is 30 
years or older. During a three decade time span, roofs should be replaced, pipes checked and updated, and retrofit 
requirements for single-wall homes or sprinkler upgrades for condos should be completed at least once. That 
includes hurricane clips115—metal connectors that reinforce the connection between a home’s roof and walls. 
These are all expensive upgrades and retrofits to make for older housing stock. 

The Marco Polo residential building in Honolulu was required to retrofit 568 units after a 2017 fire, the cost of 
which was nearly $9,000 per unit for an overall $5 million upgrade.116 To add hurricane clips to single family 
homes, prices start around $1,500 and go up depending on construction type and number of stories.117 For many 
residents, these kinds of expenses are simply not feasible.118 119 

Environmental conditions in Hawaiʻi accelerate deterioration. Termites, mold, sun, saltwater, and humidity all 
shorten the lifespan of building materials.120 The result is that much of our older housing stock has not kept 
up with evolving safety requirements or higher standards demanded by insurers. Many properties face rising 
premiums, non renewals, or limited coverage—not because residents have done anything wrong, but because the 
buildings themselves are aging out of compliance.

Facing a dual challenge, Hawaiʻi must retrofit its older housing for climate resilience—a costly but essential task—
while ensuring new construction meets modern codes from the start. Strengthening existing homes protects 
current communities and affordability, while building resilient new housing secures a safer, more insurable future 
for all.

Actionable tools that the state and counties can use to build resilient communities, upgrade both older and newer 
housing stock, and address moral hazard in insurance markets include:

•  Code Updates and Resilience Overlays. Fast-track adoption of the latest hazard-specific building codes, 

moving urgently from 2018 codes to current international standards and tailor amendments for regional risks 
such as hurricanes, wildfires and coastal flooding.121  

Develop overlay zones within local zoning maps, applying stricter resilience standards, including elevation, 
fire resistance and hurricane protection in areas with the highest environmental risk.122

•  Retrofit Incentive Programs & Anti-Displacement Provisions. Expand state and county tax credits 

specifically for upgrades like hurricane clips, fire-resistant roofing, elevation, and pest/moisture mitigation.  

Establish and fully fund grants or low-interest loans for multifamily properties to facilitate sprinkler retrofits, 
resilient utility upgrades, climate-proof heating/cooling, and full roof and plumbing replacements.  

Pair incentives with robust anti-displacement protections, such as requiring tenant habitability plans, 
temporary relocation assistance, and rent stabilization during retrofit work.

•  Education & Community Partnerships. Support and scale up partnerships with organizations like the Hawaiʻi 
Wildfire Management Organization and local resilience offices to provide homeowner and tenant education on 
environmental risks, preparedness, and strategies for building resilience.123  

Include resilience education for planners, homebuilders, realtors, landlords, tenants and homeowners, 
ensuring widespread understanding of mitigation resources, local programs to build resilience, and 
insurance requirements.
