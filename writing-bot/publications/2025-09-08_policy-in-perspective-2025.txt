Hawaiʻi Appleseed is committed to a more socially and economically just Hawaiʻi, where everyone has genuine 
opportunities to achieve economic security and fulfill their potential. We change systems to address inequity 
and foster greater opportunity by conducting data analysis and research to address income inequality, educating 
policymakers and the public, engaging in collaborative problem solving and coalition building, and advocating 
for policy and systems change.

The work of Hawaiʻi Appleseed is about people. The issues we work on—housing, food, wages, mobility, the state 
budget and taxation, and racial and indigenous equity—are important because they ensure people have access 
to shelter, sustenance, and the means to survive and thrive individually and collectively. Addressing these issues 
requires the knowledge and expertise of the people that have first-hand experience and live with the adverse 
consequences of our flawed systems.

2 • POLICY IN PERSPECTIVE

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

THE 2025 LEGISLATIVE SESSION convened under twin existential 
threats: Hawaiʻi’s worsening affordability crisis, and an impending fiscal 
tsunami of federal budget cuts. With Congress poised to slash Medicaid, 
SNAP, and Section 8 housing vouchers—programs hundreds of thousands 
of residents depend on—lawmakers faced a stark policy choice: raise 
state revenue through new taxes, or reactively cut services to avoid them.

Hawaiʻi’s experience during the Great Recession reveals the devastating 
community and economic costs of austerity policy.1 Decades of research 
further disproves the notion that low taxes on the wealthy create shared 
prosperity; so-called “trickle-down” economic policies drain community 
wealth to boost shareholder profits, leaving the public with fewer 
resources and fewer opportunities.2

Federal policy now doubles down on this failed framework. It is therefore 
critical that Hawaiʻi moves in the opposite direction—offsetting federal 
tax cuts for the rich with state taxes on wealth. This would not only 
combat rising inequality, a key driver of societal challenges,3 4 but also 
help fill funding gaps caused by federal cuts, preserving essential services 
and avoiding the deep harm of austerity.

What emerged by session’s end was a series of courageous but 
incomplete victories, alongside missed opportunities. The 2025 session 
laid bare both the power of organized communities and the paralyzing 
grip of systemic inequity.

Contradiction in Outcomes

• 

Food Equity: Thanks to the tireless work of coalition partners and overwhelming public support,8 a landmark 
school meal expansion law, Act 139, will end lunch-line stigma and feed 11,000 additional keiki this year. By 
2027, when fully phased in, tens of thousands more children will receive free meals. This is a decisive victory 
for students, parents, and our community—but our work is not done. The state must continue expanding 
access until every student is included.

•  Transportation Equity: Together, Act 212 and Act 112 are projected to boost Safe Routes to School funding 
to $5–6 million annually. This will protect keiki and the broader community by funding critical infrastructure 
improvements in high-fatality corridors near schools. Yet, legislators continue to criminalize pedestrians,  
rejecting jaywalking reform—a failure of policy underscored by tens of thousands of punitive tickets that 
have done nothing to improve street safety.9

•  Affordable Housing: Act 159 will accelerate approvals for affordable housing in Transit-Oriented 

Development zones and create a new special fund to support mixed-income projects. However, the funding 
source for this new account remains unclear. Separately, Act 278 provides a partial lifeline by requiring 
mediation before eviction, yet lawmakers again failed to fund critical rent relief—a cornerstone of successful 
mediation.10 Finally, by rejecting a locals-only housing market and conveyance tax reform without debate, 
the legislature failed to secure a competitive edge for local residents.

•  Tax & Budget Policy: Tourists will now contribute more to preserving Hawaiʻi’s natural environment and 
infrastructure through the “Green Fee,” a Transient Accommodation Tax hike established by Act 096. Yet, 
lawmakers continue to shield the ultra-wealthy, rejecting tens of millions in annual revenue by preserving a 
loophole that taxes capital gains at a lower rate than workers’ salaries. In rejecting a state Child Tax Credit, 
they denied struggling families proven relief and abandoned a powerful tool to fight poverty and stimulate 
our economy.

Cross-Cutting Tensions

1.  Grassroots Power vs. Fiscal Reality: Grassroots advocacy, like powerful student testimony against lunch 

shaming, successfully pushed lawmakers to expand school meals. However, state underfunding and federal 
cuts prevented a universal program, forcing an incremental solution even as trade policies drive costs higher 
for families.

2.  Reactive Scarcity vs. Transformative Vision: Lawmakers rightly set aside a $200 million contingency fund 

for looming federal cuts. But the amount—enough to run the state for just four days—is insufficient to cover a 
sustained loss of Medicaid and SNAP funds. This highlights the crisis of dependence on an unreliable federal 
government. We can change this by fairly taxing wealth, businesses, and visitors to transform our state, yet 
lawmakers continue to reject these reforms. 

3.  Human Need vs. Entrenched Privilege: Nowhere is the conflict between a people-first Hawaiʻi and a profit-

driven one more stark than in housing. Public housing tenants, facing uncertain futures after redevelopment, 
pleaded for assurance and were sent away empty-handed. Meanwhile, investors continue to park wealth in our 
real estate, driving up costs and risking our keiki’s future for their own profit.

This session proved Hawaiʻi can protect its people—if elected leaders choose courage over complacency.

FOOD EQUITY

To achieve true food equity in Hawaiʻi, we must confront the systemic barriers that create the daily reality of 
hunger and poor nutrition for many families.

•  Nearly 1 in 3 Hawaiʻi households struggle with food insecurity.11 Among food insecure households with keiki, 
child food insecurity was estimated at 29 percent, but food insecurity among adults in these households was 
almost 10 points higher, at 38 percent.12 

•  Over 40 percent of Native Hawaiians, other Pacific Islanders, Filipinos, and American Indian/Alaska Natives in 

Hawaiʻi live in food insecure households.13 

These figures are staggering, and dramatically underscore the harmful impacts of colonialism on native food 
systems and nutritional access. 

Meanwhile, demand at Hawaiʻi’s food banks remains higher than before the pandemic at the same time that 
Hawaiʻi has lost millions of dollars in federal emergency food assistance funding.14 Looming federal SNAP cuts are 
expected to make this gap even more severe, leaving more families at risk of going hungry.

Act 139 (Senate Bill 1300): School Meal Expansion

• 

• 

Establishes a three-year process to phase-in free school meal coverage for keiki from households making less 
than 300 percent of the Federal Poverty Level ($93,600 for a family of four). Beginning in School Year (SY) 2025, 
the new policy eliminates reduced-price fees. Full phase-in will occur in SY2027.

Effective immediately, the bill also bans “lunch shaming” by prohibiting meal denials over unpaid balances, 
ending a cruel and unnecessary trauma experienced by as many as 12,000 Hawaiʻi students annually.

•  While the policy does not yet offer universal coverage, Governor Josh Green publicly stated at the bill signing 
ceremony that his administration is committed to guiding the state toward achieving universal coverage over 
the next five years.

Act 264 (SB960): SNAP Justice

• 

• 

•

Allocates $1.1 million per year (for two years) for increased staffing and initiatives at the Hawaiʻi Department 
of Human Services (DHS) to improve SNAP participation. Strengthening the workforce behind SNAP will help 
ensure that thousands of families who rely on this critical program are able to put food on the table.

An additional measure, House Concurrent Resolution 146, urges the Hawaiʻi Department of Human Services 
to pursue federal Elderly Simplified Application Project (ESAP) waivers—a means of further simplifying 
applications for some 15,000 Hawaiʻi seniors who struggle with food insecurity.

Act 250 (House Bill 300): Farm to Food Bank Funding

The operating budget bill for the state’s executive branch, HB300 directs $500,000 per year for the next two 
years to Hawaiʻi’s Farm to Food Bank program—an initiative that supports both local farmers and families 
facing food insecurity. 

The funding will be used to purchase fresh, local, and culturally relevant food directly from Hawaiʻi farmers to 
distribute to households that need it most.

These steps forward signal that the legislature has finally begun to take seriously the threat of food insecurity. 
However, far more is possible. Too often, hunger is still seen as a matter of charity rather than as a structural 
challenge that demands system-wide solutions. By treating food security as a human right and aligning our 
policies with that value, we can build a future where every child, kūpuna, and family has consistent access to the 
nourishment they need to thrive.

TRANSPORTATION 
EQUITY

Equitable access to transportation—and the mobility that comes with it—is more than a question of convenience. 
It is a fundamental need for survival. However, our current transportation system does not provide equitable 
access to mobility.

•  Our transportation system is heavily reliant on vehicle travel, and fails to deliver equitable access to those who 
do not drive. This system creates unsustainable levels of traffic, negatively impacting the health and quality of 
life of residents, and contributing heavily to climate degradation.15 Often it is the most vulnerable who pay the 
heaviest price for this flawed system, including keiki, kūpuna and rural residents. 

• 

In rural Hawaiʻi County, Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders are 300 percent more likely to die in vehicle 
collisions compared to the state average.16 Honolulu’s densely-populated, urban areas, with large mixed-Asian, 
Native Hawaiian, and Pacific Island populations, are hotspots for jaywalking citations compared to lower-
density, higher income suburban areas with larger Asian and White populations.17

Legislative Breakdown

Act 212 (HB1231) and Act 112 (SB1009): Safe Routes to School Funding

• 

An additional $5 fee added to vehicle registration, as well as new fines for illegal parking, will generate an 
estimated $5–6 million per year for the severely under-funded Safe Routes to School (SRTS) program. The 
program funds pedestrian and roadway safety improvements within a 1-mile radius of any public school. The 
vast majority of Hawaiʻi residents live within 1 mile of a public school. 

•  While this funding is a welcome start, it will barely scratch the surface of an estimated $1 billion multi-modal 

infrastructure backlog throughout Hawaiʻi.18

Act 140 (HB862): Student Transit Lifeline

• 

• 

Authorizes the use of vans and motorcoaches during bus driver shortages. This law codifies a 2024 emergency 
proclamation made by Governor Green in the wake of the Hawaiʻi State Department of Education (DOE) 
suspending over 100 bus routes across the state due to a shortage of drivers. The lack of service is again 
especially burdensome to rural, lower-income, and Native Hawaiian households and children.

The DOE’s contract with its current bus service provider doesn’t allow for the use of alternative vehicles 
besides standard yellow buses. The contract will need to be amended before this law can begin to address 
the problem.19

TRANSPORTATION EQUITY

SB106: Jaywalking Reform

•  More than 30,000 jaywalking citations were issued in Hawaiʻi between 2018–2022, nearly all within the City 
& County of Honolulu. These citations drained $3.8 million from the pockets of people in mostly poorer 
communities while doing nothing to reduce traffic collisions with pedestrians.20

•  Despite a diverse coalition of supporters for SB106, the bill died. The police union continues to frame 

necessary reform as “lawlessness.” This position ignores both the data around the effectiveness of jaywalking 
citations, as well as the data confirming unequal enforcement targeting lower income communities and 
resulting in racist outcomes.21

The legislature made only a fraction of the progress that is needed to address the imbalance in our transportation 
system that comes from extreme reliance on vehicle travel. The harm that overreliance causes will continue 
until action is taken to shift resources within the Hawaiʻi Department of Transportation (HDOT)’s budget toward 
projects that reduce vehicle travel and create walkable communities.22

AFFORDABLE HOUSING

Hawaiʻi’s ongoing affordable housing crisis was once again a central topic of discussion at the state capitol in 2025, 
generating a large and diverse array of measures. The story of local household displacement drives the discourse.

• 

The median single-family home in Hawaiʻi now costs $950,000—affordable only to households making nearly 
$190,000 a year and far above what most Hawaiʻi families earn. 23 The median condo now costs $600,000 and 
comes with rising HOA fees and insurance costs.24 With construction at historic lows and investors buying up 
more and more properties, residents are being priced out and pushed into unstable housing situations.

•  Hawaiʻi has the second highest housing wage in the U.S. That’s the wage needed to afford the median rent for 
a two-bedroom, market rate apartment without spending more than 30 percent of one’s income. The National 
Low Income Housing Coalition puts Hawaiʻi’s housing wage at $49.19 an hour in 2025, just $0.49 cents below 
California.25 With Hawaiʻi’s minimum wage at $14 (soon to be $16) an hour, a minimum wage worker would 
need to work 140 hours a week—or 3.5 jobs—to afford the median rent without becoming cost-burdened.26

Legislative Breakdown

Act 159 (HB1409): Transit-Oriented Development

•  Creates a subaccount within the Rental Housing Revolving Fund for affordable housing development within 1 
mile of rail transit stations for the Honolulu Skyline. Together with the creation of a mandatory county transit 
zone policy, this could unlock thousands of desperately-needed units within the Honolulu urban core by 2035.

• 

• 

• 

• 

Act 268 (HB422): School Impact Fee Reform

Eliminates the construction cost component of school impact fees—a long-standing burden on housing 
development. These fees, often passed on to buyers and renters, have historically driven up housing costs 
without delivering new schools. Their removal could meaningfully reduce development expenses.

Act 278 (SB825): Eviction Mediation

Extends the period for a notice of termination of a rental agreement from five business days to 10 calendar 
days, which adds a maximum of just three additional business days with which a tenant has to work with 
government agencies or private entities, which are typically closed on the weekends.

The law also implements a two-year landlord-tenant mediation program modeled on Act 57 (2021). Mediation 
prior to summary action is mandatory as long as a tenant files for it within 10 calendar days.

Act 309 (HB431): Funding Homeless Services

Provides $50 million in funding for the Kauhale Initiative (which creates communal housing with support for 
resident-led initiatives) and for a permanent version of the ʻOhana Zones program. These programs offer more 
than temporary shelter—they provide pathways to stability through critical support and case management.

HB739 and HB740: Creating a Locals-Only Housing Market

•  Despite successful models in places like Vail, Colorado—where a locals-only market reduced housing costs by 

20 percent—lawmakers shelved similar policies for Hawaiʻi. 

• 

• 

• 

• 

• 

These bills offered a pragmatic solution to shield Hawaiʻi’s limited housing supply from speculative 
investors—a key factor in the state’s $1 million median home price. Less than a quarter of Hawaiʻi residents 
can afford that median price. Yet, despite support from businesses, nonprofits and government agencies, 
neither measure crossed the finish line.

HB1410: Conveyance Tax Reform

After years of refinement, this proposal would have directly funded affordable housing development by raising 
taxes on luxury and investment properties, bringing down overall housing costs for all residents.27 The bill 
didn’t receive a single hearing in the Senate—a stark reminder of how political inertia protects the status quo.

HB432: Missing Middle Workforce Housing 

The state’s affordable housing programs largely serve either very low-income residents (through Low-Income 
Housing Tax Credit projects) or those earning up to 140 percent Area Median Income (AMI).28 That leaves 
teachers, nurses and civil servants—who usually don’t make enough to reach 140 percent AMI—stranded in the 
middle. HB432 sought to fill this gap by funding rentals for households earning 80–120 percent AMI. This bill 
was also shelved.

HB1325: Renter’s Right to Return

This bill would have guaranteed the tenant’s “right to return” to their unit after renovations. The bill would 
have protected vulnerable renters everywhere from being pushed out of their units when redevelopment 
occurs. It would have also specifically addressed the situation of tenants living in Kuhio Park Terrace (KPT), a 
public housing project slated for redevelopment. The tenants at KPT were the leading voice in championing 
the bill, showing up to testify and lobby despite busy schedules.

The power imbalance between tenants and landlords continues to drive housing instability. Yet—for years 
now—efforts to strengthen tenant’s rights have been sidelined at the legislature.29 Bills to establish just-cause 
eviction standards (HB466), extend eviction notice periods (HB464), and seal eviction records (HB463) all 
stalled, leaving renters vulnerable to sudden displacement still.

The severity of the housing crisis in Hawaiʻi demands more than adjustments to existing policy frameworks. 
Going forward, the legislature must think bigger and bolder when it comes to housing policy, and embrace 
transformative policies it rejected in 2025.

TAX & BUDGET

Hawaiʻi’s tax system functions as an economic drain rather than as an engine. The system unfairly burdens 
the working class despite its limited capacity, while the wealthy consume vast resources without proportional 
contribution. Specifically, the state relies heavily on the General Excise Tax (GET), which takes a larger share of 
income from those already struggling to make ends meet than it does from the wealthy. The GET drives up the 
cost of living and stifles economic activity. A more fair and prosperous system would instead better leverage taxes 
on wealth and the profits of large corporations to fund investments in communities through the state budget.

• 

The average effective state and local tax rate for the bottom 40 percent of households in Hawaiʻi is over 14 
percent, but the top 1 percent of households in Hawaiʻi pay an effective tax rate of just over 10 percent.30

•  Multinational corporations make huge profits from the business activity they conduct in Hawaiʻi, while 

dodging the taxes they should be paying to support our state. Corporations do this by moving the profits 
earned within Hawaiʻi to tax havens in foreign countries that levy almost no corporate taxes. Hawaiʻi loses an 
estimated $85 million per year from this type of tax evasion.31

• 

• 

In 2024, the state slashed income taxes through Act 046. However, the vast majority of the benefits will flow to 
the wealthy, while the average household in Hawaiʻi will get comparatively little relief. At the same time, this 
broad cut strategy will cost the state an estimated $1.4 billion each year by 2031.32

Federal cuts suggest an even bigger tax and budget disaster is on the way, should Hawaiʻi have to pay more 
to keep Medicaid, SNAP and Section 8 functioning. Such deep cuts to healthcare, food security and housing 
would be devastating to the economic and social wellbeing of Hawaiʻi and its communities. The cuts  would be 
inflicted to provide even more tax cuts to the rich, a dangerous escalation of failed “trickle-down” policy.

Given these budget problems and existing inequity within our tax code, lawmakers had many reasons and many 
opportunities to make improvements and prepare for difficult times ahead. However, for the most part, bold tax 
reform was not a priority for lawmakers in 2025.

TAX & BUDGET

HB1369: Recapturing GET Revenue from Businesses

• 

The General Excise Tax (GET) is the largest source of revenue for the state’s general fund, accounting for 
roughly half of all revenues collected. Hawaiʻi exempts some businesses from paying GET in certain situations 
to help stimulate economic activity. This bill proposed eliminating several exemptions to the GET for some 
businesses to recapture some of that lost revenue.

•  While the legislature adopted a final version of the bill, Governor Green vetoed it.

HB476: Closing the Capital Gains Loophole

•  Hawaiʻi is one of the few states that taxes capital gains at a far lower rate (7.25 percent) than its top income 

tax rate (11 percent). This creates a loophole: the wealthiest individuals, who are most able to invest, can park 
money in capital assets like stocks, bonds, antiques, art, real estate and other luxury goods to leverage this 
lower rate and reduce their effective tax burden.

•  While an ideal bill would simply apply the same marginal tax rates to capital gains as the state does with its 
regular income tax, HB476 would have at least raised the capital gains tax rate to 9 percent. This would have 
recaptured an estimated $84.4 million (FY 2026)34 in taxes on investment profits assessed to those households 
making more than $400,000 per year—only about 0.3 percent of Hawaiʻi’s absolute richest households. 

• 

• 

• 

• 

The bill passed committee but was then recommitted on the Senate floor. This legislative maneuver returned 
the measure to committee, where it would need another hearing. With deadlines passed, this action effectively 
ended the proposal for the year.

HB694: A Hawaiʻi Child Tax Credit 

The federal Child Tax Credit (CTC) is one of the most effective tools the government has to alleviate childhood 
poverty. During the height of the pandemic recession, the federal government expanded its CTC and childhood 
poverty dropped by an astounding 40 percent.35 The next year, after the expansion was allowed to expire, 
childhood poverty shot back up to pre-expansion levels.36 

The bill would have supplemented the federal CTC by providing a state level credit to an estimated 176,000 
children in low- and middle-income households.37 The credit would have started at $650 per child for families 
earning under $40,000 and would have phased out completely for those earning $115,000 or more.

Alleviating poverty not only creates a more just society, it also creates a more resilient and prosperous 
economy as more dollars are circulated through increased economic activity. The entire cost of less than $84 
million could likely have been paid for through the capital gains tax increase in HB476.

Other tax reforms that did not advance:

•  SB313: A proposed 1 percent wealth tax on fortunes larger than $20 million.

•  HB759: A bill to close the loophole that lets multinational corporations avoid Hawaiʻi taxes by shifting profits 

to offshore havens.

Hawaiʻi’s tax system overburdens struggling families and rewards the wealthy with loopholes. Yet in 2025, 
lawmakers did little to fix it. Consequently, low-income families will remain trapped by a disproportionate tax 
burden, and the wealthy will continue shifting their gains into off-island accounts and asset portfolios. This failure 
to act serves no one but the wealthy few, and harms our local economy and communities in the process.

LOOKING FORWARD

While this year’s wins have laid important groundwork for a path toward a better future, state policymakers must 
embrace transformational reforms and bold community investments going forward—especially in the face of 
federal funding cuts. Austerity and “trickle-down economics” must be rejected.

Momentum toward universal free school meals, initial investments in the Safe Routes to School program, funding 
for homeless services, policy to streamline urban housing development, as well as the green fee on tourism to 
fund environmental protection and infrastructure maintenance are all promising initiatives.

However, lawmakers missed a chance to create a locals-only housing market and reform the conveyance tax to 
deter speculation, lower costs for locals, and fund housing development. Renters in particular were left with 
neither workforce housing support nor critical tenant protections—a continuation of years of neglect. Even 
mandatory eviction mediation, while helpful, is far less effective without rental assistance, which the legislature 
chose not to fund.

Similarly, lawmakers missed a chance to collect tens of millions from the wealthy and corporations, and to provide 
meaningful tax relief to struggling families. Tip-toeing around tax reform is no longer an option. With federal 
policies threatening to raise the cost of living even further, bold and transformative change is unavoidable. This 
must include the courage to reconsider recent broad tax cuts and instead reinvest in targeted credits that truly 
stabilize working families.

Progress is possible when community power demands it. The resilience of advocates and impacted residents 
testifying at the legislature is what lays the foundation for change. To counter well-funded opposition and achieve 
the transformational reform Hawaiʻi needs, we must continue to build that power, centering the voices of the 
community in every policy debate. Our future depends on it.
