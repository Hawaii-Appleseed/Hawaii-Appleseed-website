Hawaiʻi Appleseed is committed to a more socially and economically just Hawaiʻi, where everyone has genuine 
opportunities to achieve economic security and fulfill their potential. We change systems to address inequity 
and foster greater opportunity by conducting data analysis and research to address income inequality, educating 
policymakers and the public, engaging in collaborative problem solving and coalition building, and advocating 
for policy and systems change.

The work of Hawaiʻi Appleseed is about people. The issues we work on—housing, food, wages, mobility, the state 
budget and taxation, and racial and indigenous equity—are important because they ensure people have access 
to shelter, sustenance, and the means to survive and thrive individually and collectively. Addressing these issues 
requires the knowledge and expertise of the people that have first-hand experience and live with the adverse 
consequences of our flawed systems.

INTRODUCTION: 
WHY COUNTY 
ACTION MATTERS

COUNTIES HAVE A CRITICAL ROLE to play in strengthening food security, especially as Hawaiʻi faces rising costs 
of living and high rates of food insecurity. 

According to recent numbers, food insecurity is a pressing challenge across the islands: Hawaiʻi Island faces the 
greatest impact, where 43 percent of households are affected. Sizeable proportions in Maui (41 percent), Kauaʻi 
(32 percent), and Oʻahu (25 percent) also report difficulty accessing enough food.1 

At the same time, shifting federal priorities and proposed cuts to nutrition and health programs threaten to 
reduce the reliability of resources that low-income families rely on, including the Supplemental Nutrition 
Assistance Program (SNAP) and Medicaid.

Since 2018, each county has added a dedicated Food Access Coordinator (FAC), partially funded by the SNAP-
Education program and administered through the Hawaiʻi Department of Health.2 These FACs play a central 
role in assessing community needs, leading the development of county-wide Food Systems Plans, and 
mapping emergency food-response efforts.3 In 2025, Congress cut SNAP-Ed funding with the passage of H.R.1. 
Without stable funding for these critical positions, communities risk losing the backbone of their local food 
security infrastructure.

Against this backdrop, counties are uniquely positioned to adapt and respond to smaller-scale community 
needs. Unlike federal and state programs, county-level governance has a closer connection to residents, making 
for community-focused solutions that reflect local needs. 

For instance, as the state’s most rural county,4 Hawaiʻi County’s approach to food production may reflect its 
uniquely dispersed communities and agricultural landscape. Maui County, on the other hand, is the only county 
comprising three islands, and may need to prioritize interisland transportation support for Molokaʻi and Lanaʻi to 
address geographic barriers to access. 

While this report aims to evaluate the current programs and resources that counties in Hawaiʻi can use to 
strengthen food security, these recommendations are not meant as a replacement of entire nutrition programs 
like SNAP, which are seeing historically drastic reductions in benefits and overall funding. Nor do these 
recommendations assume that counties will be shouldering these efforts alone. County efforts must be coupled 
with significant state support, and utilization of federal programs that remain strong.

ALREADY UNDERWAY

Hawaiʻi’s four counties are already taking steps to strengthen food security through the development and 
implementation of individual county food access plans or food systems plans.6 In most cases, counties already 
have the key agencies, staff and infrastructure to administer these programs. 

The high level “policy levers” and examples listed below illustrate how counties are already advancing these 
priorities, and where further investment and coordination could have the greatest impact.

Key Agency: Emergency Management Agencies

Rationale: Counties can play a vital role in disaster 
response by coordinating food distribution efforts 
before, during and after disasters.

County Emergency Management Agencies are 
well-positioned to lead or support planning that 
ensures food distribution networks are ready to 
respond to crises—from wildfires to economic 
shocks These agencies should strengthen their 
connections with schools, food banks, and 
community organizations. Disaster preparation is 
most effective when it’s part of broader resilience

SUMMER FEEDING PROGRAMS

Key Agency: Departments of Parks and Recreation

Rationale: Summer meal programs provide a critical bridge by ensuring children continue to receive the nutrition 
they need when school is not in session.

Across Hawaiʻi, Departments of Parks and Recreation (DPRs) in every county have, at one point, sponsored the 
federally-reimbursed Summer Food Service Program (SFSP) (See Appendix I).17 These departments administer 
summer programs at parks and community centers, usually partnering with a local food vendor or the Department 
of Education to provide daily meals for children, whether they are enrolled in the program or not. 

Counties can strengthen summer feeding systems by continuing to expand coverage to underserved areas or 
coordinating with partners to offer non-congregate options (such as a weekly food box) in rural areas.

DIRECT SUPPORT FOR FOOD BANKS

Key Agency: Varies

Rationale: Food banks play a critical role, especially for people who don’t qualify for other types of food 
assistance. They are often seen as a “last line of defense” for families when no other options are available. While 
federal programs often provide a baseline of support, these charitable organizations tend to rely largely on 
donations.29 Funding from both the state and county can enable a more flexible response to the steadily increasing 
need that food banks are seeing as food costs rise.

Although it is not yet common in Hawaiʻi, counties can directly support these critical institutions, tailoring 
programs to each county’s need. County funds can be targeted to help expand food distribution, purchase fresh or 
culturally relevant foods, support local farmers, and strengthen infrastructure like cold storage and transportation.

WHAT COUNTIES 
CAN DO NOW

Each county’s respective food access plan provides the 
overarching framework for action. Counties already 
have the agencies, staff and infrastructure to play a 
stronger role in food security. Building on these plans 
and existing programs, a few priorities stand out as 
especially actionable:

1.  Standardize and strengthen county multi-agency 

feeding support plans. Several counties are already 
developing comprehensive multi-agency plans with 
connected task forces to improve preparation for 
disaster feeding. Counties without them should follow 
suit, ensuring clear roles, pre-arranged agreements, 
and partnerships with local suppliers and relevant 
feeding agencies. 

2.  Provide direct, flexible support to food banks. 

Reliable county funding can stabilize core food bank 
operations, expand fresh and culturally-preferred 
food purchasing, and strengthen infrastructure like 
cold storage and transportation.

3.  Expand kūpuna and keiki feeding programs. Building 

on the Area Agencies on Aging and Department 
of Parks and Recreation programs, counties can 
extend reach by utilizing all federal programs eligible 
to them (SFSP, CSFP, CACFP, Summer EBT) while 
investing more in home-delivered meals, congregate 
sites, and keiki summer feeding.

4.  Maximize federal nutrition programs at the local 
level. Counties can boost participation in federal 
programs such as SNAP, WIC and DA BUX by funding 
outreach, supporting application assistance, and 
expanding vendor capacity to accept benefits.

CONCLUSION

Counties across Hawaiʻi face both pressing challenges and powerful opportunities in the fight against food 
insecurity. With federal nutrition programs under threat and state resources stretched thin, counties cannot afford 
to remain passive. By implementing existing food access plans, investing in emergency preparedness, supporting 
food banks, and strengthening kūpuna and keiki feeding programs, counties can make immediate, tangible 
improvements in residents’ daily lives.

At the same time, county action is not a substitute for robust federal and state support. Rather, it is a 
complementary strategy—one that grounds statewide food security goals in the realities of local communities. 
Counties are closest to the people they serve, and that proximity allows them to innovate, partner, and act with 
agility. By seizing these levers, Hawaiʻi’s counties can not only buffer their residents against federal funding 
uncertainty but also build more resilient, equitable food systems for the future.
