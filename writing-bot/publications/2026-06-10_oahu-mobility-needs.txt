and foster greater opportunity by conducting data analysis and research to address income inequality, educating 
policymakers and the public, engaging in collaborative problem solving and coalition building, and advocating 
for policy and systems change.

requires the knowledge and expertise of the people that have first-hand experience and live with the adverse 
consequences of our flawed systems.

2 • MOBILITY NEEDS

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

THE CURRENT, MAINSTREAM understanding of how people get around on Oʻahu is largely defined by commuter 
data. However, this data only captures a fraction of our island’s total mobility. This report analyzes the results of 
Hawaiʻi Appleseed’s 2025 Oʻahu Travel Survey, which we used to better understand how residents access school, 
grocery stores, medical appointments, and other destinations that do not fit into the standard 9-to-5 work day.

The survey findings reveal a significant mobility gap. While personal vehicles remain the primary mode to 
access essential and recreational destinations, many households lack access to safe and affordable alternatives. 
Alarmingly, the survey found that: 

•  Half of all residents report that transportation costs negatively impact their ability to reach essential and 

recreational destinations.

•  Nearly 1 in 4 Oʻahu residents faced transportation issues so severe in the past month that they were unable to 

leave their homes.

This report urges decision makers, as well as municipal agencies responsible for the planning and design of 
Hawaiʻi roadways, to shift their focus away from vehicle-centered infrastructure and toward transportation 
that puts people first. More specifically, the report’s key recommendations include improving pedestrian 
infrastructure and safety, expanding affordable and reliable transit options, and expanding student 
transportation options.

LIMITATIONS OF 
COMMUTER DATA

Our most comprehensive data from the U.S. Census has historically only included data on time traveled to work, 
and commuting mode. While 2024 commute mode data shows that 63.7 percent of Oʻahu’s workforce drives 
alone to work, these statistics capture only a fraction of the island’s total mobility (Figure 1).

Census data does provide some insight on how people of different ages, gender, and incomes commute to 
work differently (Figure 2). However, the 2025 Oʻahu Travel Survey moves beyond basic commuter statistics 
to investigate how diverse factors—including geographic region, household income, disability status, age, 
and ethnicity—influence transportation choices for all aspects of daily life. By analyzing these intersecting 
demographics, the survey sought to identify specific mobility gaps and challenges that standard employment-
based data often overlooks.

SURVEY 
METHODOLOGY

The Oʻahu Travel survey was completed with the financial support of the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation and 
Urban Institute’s Local Data for Equitable Communities grant. It was administered in collaboration with Ward 
Research. The survey, collected between September 4–October 31, 2025, reached a total of 666 respondents. 
Surveys were shared through Ward Research’s online panel and Hawaiʻi Appleseed’s network. The surveys were 
completed via mail (181) and online (485). Data was weighted to the Oʻahu population using 2023 American 
Community Survey data.

Profile of Respondents

The following section details the key demographic characteristics of survey participants. The demographic 
makeup of the survey respondents is generally representative of Oʻahu residents, with the exception of keiki 
and young adults. Due to the limitations in surveying young people, the travel survey included questions about 
student transportation challenges and opportunities for survey respondents with school-aged children.

Age: The 25-34 age bracket represented 26 percent of respondents, with a steady distribution across middle-
aged and senior cohorts. Gender distribution was nearly equal, with 49 percent identifying as men and 48 
percent as women.

GENERAL MOBILITY 
PATTERNS

Across Oʻahu, personal vehicles remain the primary mode of travel for both essential and recreational trips. 
While most residents are generally mobile, they frequently encounter barriers that make daily travel difficult or 
even impossible.

Dominance of Personal Vehicles

Beyond the traditional commute to work, personal vehicles are the primary mode for both essential and 
recreational travel across Oʻahu. While the vast majority of residents depend on their own vehicles to 
reach medical appointments and grocery stores, a significant portion of the community also rely on public 
transportation, walking/wheelchairs, carpooling, and bikes/micromobility for these vital trips (Table 7).

Although personal vehicles remain the primary mode of transport to reach all recreational locations, 
respondents are more likely to walk or carpool to recreational sites than to essential destinations (Table 8).

MOBILITY NEEDS • 11

Personal vehicles are most used by residents in Central Oʻahu, those aged 45–64, and individuals of Native 
Hawaiian/Pacific Islander or Japanese descent. Conversely, the use of public transportation is more prevalent 
among residents aged 18–44, individuals of Filipino descent, and individuals living in households with annual 
incomes below $75,000.

Regional infrastructure also dictates these transportation behaviors. For example, residents in Central Oʻahu 
and Windward/North Shore are significantly more likely to report that they never walk to their destinations 
compared to those in Urban Honolulu. This suggests that for many, car dependency is less a preference and more 
a necessity dictated by their physical environment.

Primary Transportation Challenges

Traffic congestion and long drive times emerged as the primary transportation concern across all demographics, 
with nearly half of all survey participants citing these as major issues (Figure 3). This concern was particularly 
salient among respondents living in Urban Honolulu, Central Oʻahu, and ʻEwa/Leeward, as well as those in 
middle-income households. 

Beyond congestion and drive times, survey respondents also identified safety and reliability as significant 
mobility barriers. More specifically, a substantial portion of respondents pointed to unsafe routes, such as a lack 
of sidewalks or poorly maintained roads, as a challenge. This concern regarding unsafe or nonexistent pedestrian 
infrastructure was disproportionately mentioned by residents of Urban Honolulu and Windward/North Shore. 

Furthermore, while some residents reported that public transit is too unreliable or infrequent to meet their 
needs, others identified the high cost of transportation as a critical barrier. This financial burden was referenced 
significantly more by women, respondents with disabilities, those in low-income households, and individuals 
identifying as Hawaiian, Pacific Islander or Filipino.

THE MOBILITY GAP: 
INFRASTRUCTURE AND 
ACCESS

Only 50 percent  of survey respondents agreed that their communities have enough safe and accessible 
sidewalks, bike lanes, bus stops, and bus routes (Figure 4). However, this perception of infrastructure changed 
based on regional and demographic factors. 

To explore these regional disparities further, the Oʻahu Mobility Needs StoryMap includes an interactive map 
where users can examine infrastructure perception by region alongside related layers such as transportation 
insecurity and zero-vehicle households, providing a spatial picture of how these challenges vary across the island.

MOBILITY NEEDS • 13

Regional Infrastructure Disparity

Perceptions of infrastructure on Oʻahu were starkly different between survey respondents from urban and those 
from rural communities. Respondents from urban areas tended to disagree that there are enough safe sidewalks 
and bike lanes to encourage active transportation, whereas respondents from rural areas tended to disagree that 
there are enough bus routes and stops to support transit usage in their communities. 

Moreover, respondents identifying as Caucasian and those living outside of Urban Honolulu and the Pearl City/
ʻAiea/Moanalua areas were likely to agree that there were enough sidewalks in their community to encourage 
walking. By contrast, residents of Urban Honolulu and those living in households of two or more people were 
more likely to disagree that sidewalk infrastructure in their community was sufficient. 

Regarding bike infrastructure, residents of Urban Honolulu and those identifying as Native Hawaiian/Pacific 
Islander were more likely to disagree that their communities possessed enough safe and accessible bike lanes.

Access to Vehicles

While 85 percent of survey respondents owned at least one personal vehicle, access to vehicles was not a reality 
for all respondents. The survey revealed significant disparities in car ownership exist based on income and 
physical ability. Specifically, those individuals with disabilities or those earning less than $75,000 annually were 
significantly less likely to own a vehicle. Twenty-eight percent of individuals from $75,000 income or below 
households had zero cars at their home, and 30 percent of those with a disability had zero cars at their home.

Among those who did not own a vehicle, the most prevalent barrier to car ownership was inability to afford one, 
which was cited by 29 percent of respondents who did not own a vehicle (Figure 5). Other reasons for a lack 
of individual vehicle ownership included that the respondents were sharing a vehicle with another household 
member or the absence of a valid driver’s license.

TRANSPORTATION 
INSECURITY ON OʻAHU

Transportation insecurity occurs when barriers such as a lack of vehicle access, unreliable transit, or high costs 
prevent individuals from leaving their home when they need to. This issue is a significant concern across Oʻahu. 
Nearly 1 in 4 respondents reported facing transportation challenges so severe that they were unable to leave 
their homes at some point in the past month (Figure 6). Additionally, 7 percent of respondents reported being 
unable to leave their homes for 15 or more days in the past month because of a problem with transportation.

These mobility gaps are disproportionately felt by specific populations. Respondents living in Pearl City, ʻAiea, 
Moanalua, or Central Oʻahu were more likely to experience high levels of transportation insecurity, compared 
to respondents living in other areas. Transportation insecurity also affected 50 percent of respondents living 
in households with an annual income under $75,000 and 67 percent of respondents with physical or mental 
disabilities. Additionally, 38 percent of respondents living in households with three or more people, particularly 
those with children, face similar challenges. 

The financial burden of travel is a primary driver of this trend. Half of all respondents reported that 
transportation costs, such as gas, parking, and bus fares, negatively impacted their ability to reach essential 
destinations. For 7 percent of respondents, these costs were so prohibitive that they regularly prevented them 
from attending essential activities.

STUDENT HABITS

Approximately 1 in 3 survey respondents had school-aged children in their household. While the majority of 
these respondents reported relying on a personal vehicle to get their children to and from school, student 
transportation habits varied widely by age (Figure 7).

Younger elementary students are more likely to walk to school compared to older age groups, with 25 percent 
currently utilizing this mode. At the middle school level, students show a higher tendency to utilize a school 
bus for their daily commute, at a rate of 24 percent. High school students demonstrate a higher reliance on 
alternative options, with 32 percent using TheBus and 25 percent carpooling by getting rides from friends.

Survey results indicate that many students would shift to alternative modes of transportation if specific 
infrastructure or service improvements were implemented. For example, 59 percent of students would ride a 
school bus if pick-up and drop-off locations were more convenient. Regarding public transit, approximately 50 
percent of students would increase their use of TheBus if they received free or discounted passes, had access to 
more direct routes near their school, or if more frequent routes were available. 

Active transportation also shows potential for growth, as 47 percent of students would walk to school if 
sidewalks were safer and of higher quality. Further, 39 percent of students would be inclined to bike if safer bike 
lanes were available along their commute.

RECOMMENDATIONS

The results of the travel survey indicate that current transportation infrastructure and services are not meeting 
the diverse mobility needs of Oʻahu residents. While car dependency is high, residents cite congestion and long 
drive times as their primary concern. 

Yet for many, the alternatives to driving remain insufficient, unsafe, or unaffordable. The significant percentage 
of residents facing transportation insecurity and the willingness of students to use alternative modes highlight 
a clear demand for more safe, affordable, and reliable multimodal options. To improve island-wide mobility, we 
recommend the following policies, targeted to high-need populations.

MOBILITY NEEDS • 19

1. Improve Pedestrian Infrastructure and Safety

Nearly a third of survey respondents with disabilities indicated that they could not leave their home for 15 or 
more days in the past month due to transportation challenges. To support the 12.9 percent of Oʻahu residents 
living with disabilities, the state and county must prioritize transportation infrastructure that ensures that those 
with physical and mental impairments can move safely and independently throughout their community.3

Policy efforts should focus on improving the accessibility and safety of transit services, as well as pedestrian 
infrastructure, with a specific emphasis on intersections, which can be among the most dangerous places for 
people who rely on walking or rolling.

Examples of needed improvements which disability rights groups are currently advocating for include leading 
pedestrian intervals and accessible pedestrian signals. These pedestrian improvements give people a safer 
head start and make crossings more inclusive for those with disabilities. Learn more about these low-cost safety 
solutions at https://hiappleseed.org/publications/pedestrian-head-start. 

Sidewalk continuity is also a critical component of a safe and connected pedestrian network. Survey findings 
show that only half of residents feel their communities have sufficient sidewalk infrastructure, and 47 percent of 
students would walk to school if sidewalks were safer and of higher quality.

The state and county should prioritize closing sidewalk gaps, particularly along high-traffic corridors and school 
routes, to ensure that walking is a safe and viable option for all residents.

2. Expand Affordable and Reliable Transit Options

Over half of survey respondents from households earning below $75,000 reported that transportation costs 
regularly prevented them from traveling to essential destinations, or made traveling to essential destinations 
difficult. To address this, the state and county must invest in programs that expand access to public transit and 
active modes of transportation, reducing residents' reliance on and the financial burden of personal vehicle 
ownership altogether.

Improving transit reliability is equally important. Residents cite unreliable and infrequent bus service as a 
significant barrier, even among those willing to use it. Dedicated bus-only lanes on high-traffic corridors would 
reduce travel times, improve schedule reliability, and make public transit a more competitive alternative to 
driving, benefiting riders across all income levels.

While public attention on transportation affordability is often focused on lower fuel taxes, studies show that 
these strategies only provide modest savings for consumers and can further incentivize driving, leading to future 
increased traffic congestion and pollution.4 As shown in Figure 8, investments in the following programs have 
been shown to produce much larger savings: unbundling parking and housing costs; offering electric bike rebate 
and car-sharing programs; and expanding transit-oriented and compact development to ensure that essential 
services are located within walking distance of reliable public transit.5 

In 2020, the City and County of Honolulu reduced costly parking mandates and encouraged developers to 
unbundle housing and parking through the passage of Ordinance 20-41. Learn more about the financial burdens 
caused by parking mandates and the effectiveness of Ordinance 20-41 at https://hiappleseed.org/publications/
stalled-parking-mandates-housing-costs.

3. Expand Student Transportation Options

Survey respondents from larger households with children were much more likely than those from smaller 
households without children to report not being able to leave their home due to transportation issues. Moreover, 
while the majority (72 percent) of respondents with school-aged children did have access to a family vehicle, 
less than half of respondents said that their children had the ability to walk, bike, ride a school bus or TheBus, or 
carpool with friends to school. 

To address this, the state and county should invest in programs to expand student transportation options. As 
discussed in the previous section, survey respondents expressed high-levels of support for many programs to 
improve alternative modes of transportation to school and recreational activities, such as expanding school bus 
services, receiving discounted or free bus passes, improving pedestrian and bike infrastructure, and organizing 
carpooling or ride-sharing programs.  

To further encourage a shift away from car dependency and build lifelong transit habits, Oʻahu should implement 
free transit passes for all youth. Our findings show that approximately 50 percent of students would use public 
transit more frequently if free or discounted passes were available, alongside more direct routes and convenient 
stops. By removing the financial barrier of bus fares, the state can significantly improve access to education, 
extracurricular activities, and essential services for the next generation.

There have been recent advocacy efforts for a state-wide program to allow youth to ride transit fare-free. Learn 
more about the cost and benefits of free youth transit at https://hiappleseed.org/publications/keiki-ride-free.
