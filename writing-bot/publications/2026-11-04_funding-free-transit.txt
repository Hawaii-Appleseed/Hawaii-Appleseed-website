EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

PUBLIC TRANSIT connects people to opportunity: to jobs, school, medical care, and daily life. When we remove 
the cost of a fare and develop our transit systems with reliability and accessibility in mind, we can directly 
expand who can get where they need to go. 

Hawaiʻi is one of the most expensive places to live in the United States. Housing, food, and transportation costs 
place enormous pressure on working families, and the burden falls heaviest on those with the least. In 2025, the 
public cost of the vehicle economy1 in Hawaiʻi was $14,400 per taxpayer, or $33,600 per household, regardless 
of vehicle ownership.2 The additional private cost of vehicle ownership is substantial—an extra $21,800 per 
household, per year.3 

Public transit offers a powerful alternative. A single transit trip costs a fraction of what a vehicle trip costs, and 

FUNDING FREE TRANSIT • 3

sustained investment in transit infrastructure generates substantial economic returns—an estimated $5 billion in 
GDP for every $1 billion in annual investment over 20 years.4

Yet across counties, transit ridership varies significantly. Transit ridership on Oʻahu has yet to recover to pre-
pandemic levels,5 and recent fare increases are making the problem worse. Hawaiʻi County tells a different story: 
after bus ridership fell from 663,784 passenger trips in FY2019 to 325,049 in FY2021, it not only rebounded but 
has since exceeded pre-pandemic level—a turnaround driven by the county’s shift to fare-free service.6

Fare revenue covers only a small share of what it costs to run Hawaiʻi’s bus systems. On Oʻahu, fares cover 
roughly 15 percent of TheBus’s operating costs, with the remaining 85 percent already funded through public 
revenue regardless of what riders pay.7

In February 2026, Hawaiʻi Appleseed released “Keiki Ride Free,” a policy report recommending that Hawaiʻi 
enact statewide legislation to provide free transit for all residents age 18 and under.8 This report builds on that 
foundation, estimates the cost of a universal fare-free bus transit program, and identifies funding sources the 
state can use to pay for it. 

A universal program does not replace Keiki Ride Free, rather it is a broader version of the same goal. Cost 
estimates provided in this report are broken out by both county and rider age group to preserve that option.
We estimate a statewide universal fare-free bus program would cost approximately $62.4 million annually in the 
near term, assuming 10 percent utilization on Oʻahu and 5 percent on neighbor islands. Hawaiʻi County’s Hele-On 
program, which has already been fare-free since 2022, adds no additional cost.

This report focuses specifically on public bus transit, not rail or paratransit. The report does not recommend 
fare-free access to Skyline in the near-term: the system is still in active construction and its operating picture is 
not yet settled, so we recommend revisiting rail integration in a future report.

Hawaiʻi Appleseed recommends:

• 

• 

Enacting statewide legislation establishing a permanent, universal fare-free bus transit program;

Funding it through the barrel tax, the Clean Fuel Standard (SHIFT), or the State Highway Fund, distributed to 
counties by formula;

•  Requiring county transit agencies to report ridership, cost, and utilization data annually; and

• 

Investing in service improvements alongside fare elimination.

BEST PRACTICES

Hawaiʻi County’s Hele-On system, and comparable mainland programs, such as Alburquerque’s ABQ Ride Zero Fares 
and Alexandria’s DASH, point to a consistent set of features for a successful universal fare-free bus transit program. 

Based on best practices, a universal program should include the following:

•  No pass, no enrollment, no means-testing. The program should allow transit riders to board and depart 
without paying. When anyone can board without paying, without showing a pass or proving eligibility, 
ridership increases dramatically, boarding times decrease, and administrative costs fall.9 Albuquerque's 
experience confirmed that eliminating fare infrastructure saves meaningful resources that can be redirected 
to enhancing transit access, infrastructure and services.10 Hawaiʻi County's Hele-On system operates on 
exactly this model today.

• 

Year-round, statewide access. The program should apply on all county bus transit systems, statewide, year-
round. Seasonal or school-year-only restrictions leave riders without service during summers, holidays, and 
breaks, precisely when some of the most vulnerable residents need it most.

•  Build in data collection from day one. This program should prioritize the collection of detailed  transit 

ridership data. Closing the gaps in Hawaiʻi’s transit ridership data allows for more accurate utilization and 
cost estimates to sustainably fund a fare-free bus transit program.

•  Prioritize service investments. Alongside a fare-free transit program, state and county agencies should 
invest in transit service improvements. Evidence from Alexandria, VA suggests that eliminating fares can 
increase ridership on high-frequency routes.11 State and county agencies should anticipate this and plan for 
service investments such as larger buses and more frequent service on high-demand routes, alongside fare 
elimination. Increased ridership strengthens the case for these investments and can help attract additional 
federal and local transit funding.

•  Distribute state funding to counties by formula. Funding for this program should be distributed by the state 
counties based on population, ridership, and need, rather than a flat reimbursement for lost fare revenue.

WHAT DOES IT COST?

WHAT DOES FREE BUS TRANSIT FOR EVERYONE ACTUALLY COST?

Opponents of fare-free bus transit frequently assume that fare revenue is essential to keeping transit systems 
running. It is not. In Hawaiʻi, as in most jurisdictions, fare revenue covers only a small fraction of operating costs, 
with the large majority of county bus systems funded through public revenue, regardless of what riders pay.

Estimated Cost of Universal Fare-Free Bus Transit

We estimate that a statewide universal fare-free bus program would cost approximately $62.4 million annually 
in the near term. This assumes 10 percent bus transit utilization on Oʻahu and 5 percent utilization on the 
neighbor islands. 

Hawaiʻi County is excluded because Hele-On is already fare-free for all riders statewide, so a universal policy 
adds no incremental cost there. This estimate also does not include Maui County’s already fare-free populations, 
as a universal policy would add no incremental cost for them either. Only Maui’s remaining, non-exempt riders 
are reflected in the cost figures.

It is worth noting that Hele-On’s fare-free status is not currently permanent. Hawaiʻi County has extended 
it through fare suspensions, most recently via Ordinance 25-71, and it is presently authorized only through 
December 31, 2028.12 The County extended the pilot program to maintain high ridership and to avoid installation 
costs, as fares would generate about $750,000 annually, but it would cost over $1 million to install new 
fareboxes13 (see Figure 1). 

Maui County, on the other hand, has not structured its fare-free access as a pilot or a program set to expire on a 
certain date. Free bus transit for Maui’s eligible groups is fare-free indefinitely, with no signs or indications that 
the county intends to scale back the program or reinstate fares for these groups.

Assumptions Behind These Estimates

These figures estimate foregone fare revenue, not new operating spending. Looking at Oʻahu, for example, the 
$47 million TheBus currently collects in fares is the floor, not the ceiling—since eliminating fares is expected to 
increase ridership. Understanding this, the state should budget for replacing lost fare revenue, in addition to the 
cost of new riders. 

The net cost is also likely lower than Table 1 suggests once administrative savings are counted. A universal, fare-
free system eliminates the overhead that comes with charging and managing fares in the first place. All of the 
spending toward farebox collection and maintenance, processing and re-verifying reduced-fare eligibility, issuing 
and replacing passes, fare enforcement, and other fare-related administrative tasks goes away. In Alburquerque, 
the net annual cost of its fare-free program was approximately $1.8 million against a $58 million operating 
budget, roughly 3 percent of costs, once fare-collection savings were factored in.14

What Utilization Rate Should We Expect?

Most Oʻahu residents currently get around by driving alone (a 63.7 percent Census commute share), but that 
figure only captures commuting to work, not the complete range of daily trips.15 Hawaiʻi Appleseed’s 2026 “Oʻahu 
Mobility Needs” report found that half of the county’s residents say transportation costs limit their access to 
essential destinations, and nearly one in four were unable to leave their homes in the past month due to lack of 
transportation. This suggests that the utilization estimates discussed below, drawn from mainland comparisons, 
may understate real demand for a reliable fare-free system in Hawaiʻi.

Even where fare-free transit is available, not everyone rides it. It’s worth distinguishing regular riders, people 
who use transit as a routine part of daily life, from residents who might ride only once in a while. The utilization 
rates below are meant to capture the former: how many residents would become regular riders under a fare-free 
program, not the broader (and much larger) share who might ride occasionally. 

In Albuquerque, roughly 4 percent of residents regularly use its fare-free transit, with 23,800 riders in 202316—
though service there still sits at only 64 percent of pre-pandemic levels.17 In Alexandria, a denser city with more 
frequent service, an estimated 13 percent of residents use the fare-free system with a recorded 4.5 million 
boardings in Fiscal Year 2023.18 Based on these figures, we assume 10 percent utilization on Oʻahu in the near 
term and 5 percent in Maui and Kauaʻi counties, reflecting smaller, lower-frequency systems. This is consistent 
with Hele-On, where an estimated 2–3 percent of Hawaiʻi Island residents regularly ride roughly 2,500 to 3,000 
rides per day.19

What Hawaiʻi is Already Spending on Free Bus Transit

Before considering the cost of establishing a universal fare-free transit program, it is worth noting that Hawaiʻi 
already invests significantly in free and reduced-fare transit. Beyond Hele-On, Maui County offers free bus transit 
to income-eligible households, youth, seniors, Medicare cardholders and—as of August 2026—all students and 
county employees.20 Extending fare-free access to all remaining riders in Maui County, as reflected in Table 2, 
would build on a system the county has already made mostly free.

The Hawaiʻi Department of Education’s EXPRESS program also provides free bus passes to eligible middle 
and high school students during the school year, at a cost of $1 million annually, drawn from the Department 
of Education’s budget rather than HDOT’s budget.21 Under a universal program, EXPRESS would become 
unnecessary, and its budget could be redirected to other educational priorities.

Administrative Cost Savings

Every county in Hawaiʻi currently operates its own fare collection infrastructure, reduced-fare programs, and 
eligibility verification processes. A universal program would eliminate that overhead across the board. Reduced-
fare applications, pass issuance and replacement, eligibility verification, and fare enforcement would no longer 
be needed. On Hawaiʻi Island, Hele-On already operates without fare infrastructure, offering a working example 
of what that administrative baseline looks like.

A universal program would also let HIDOE wind down the EXPRESS program entirely, freeing its $1 million budget 
for reallocation. A precise statewide estimate of total administrative savings would require a formal cost study, 
but the directional conclusion is clear: the net cost of universal fare-free bus transit is lower than what Table 1 
suggests once these savings are accounted for.

How Transit Funding Currently Works

Fare revenue is a small piece of how Hawaiʻi’s bus systems are funded. In 2024, the City and County of Honolulu’s 
transit system (TheBus) had operating costs of approximately $330 million. Passenger fares generated 
approximately $47 million, an operating deficit of roughly $283 million, or 85 percent of costs, covered through 
public funding regardless of the fare charged.22 A similar pattern held in 2022 and 2023, with fares covering less 
than 20 percent of costs each year.23 

This is not unique to Oʻahu. Nationally and across Hawaiʻi’s three other counties, fares consistently cover only a 
small share of transit operating costs, with the balance funded through public revenue regardless of ridership or 
fare levels.

Transit planning and day-to-day operations in Hawaiʻi are primarily carried out by the respective county transit 
agencies, with support from the state. It is important to distinguish between two kinds of public transit funding: 

•  Capital Funding: Pays for buses, facilities, and infrastructure and comes disproportionately from federal 

sources; and 

•  Operating Funding: Pays for the day-to-day cost of running service, including the fare revenue this report 

proposes to replace. 

Counties receive substantial federal support for capital projects, but operating costs and any fare revenue 
replacement fall mostly to state and county general and special funds. That distinction matters: federal capital 
dollars cannot simply be redirected to cover an operating cost like fare-free bus transit.

Potential Funding Sources

Option 1: Barrel Tax

Hawaiʻi’s Environmental Response, Energy, and Food Security Tax, known commonly as the Barrel Tax, was 
established in 1993 as a per-barrel tax on petroleum products. Initially 5 cents per barrel to fund oil-spill 
response,24 it was later raised to its current rate of $1.05 per barrel in 2010, generating roughly $27 million to $30 
million per year.25 Table 3 breaks down how the $1.05 per barrel tax is currently allocated.

This existing tax is the primary mechanism the Legislature considered using in the 2026 session to fund a 
statewide fare-free program for keiki. If passed, HB1879/SB2699 would have directed 28 cents of the per-barrel 
petroleum tax and 7 cents of the non-petroleum fossil fuel tax into a dedicated free youth transit fund, estimated

•  Advantages: The barrel tax is already established and would not require development of a new revenue 

source. The tax would simply require legislative reallocation, potentially from the General Fund or through 
the Hydrogen Fueling System Subaccount and the Electric Vehicle Charging System Subaccount, which have 
not yet been utilized. Further, it ties transportation funding to petroleum consumption, the same activity 
fare-free bus transit is meant to reduce.

• 

Trade-offs: Barrel tax revenue is currently allocated to the general fund, in addition to a range of other 
programs; thus, allocating a portion of the barrel tax to be used for fare-free transit would mean diverting 
revenue from those existing uses, reducing their funding unless the tax rate were also increased. Barrel tax 
revenue also fluctuates with fuel prices and consumption, which could make it an unstable long-term base 
for an ongoing operating cost.

Option 2: Clean Fuel Standard Revenue

As part of the Sustainable Hawaiʻi Initiative for Fuel Transformation (SHIFT), Hawaiʻi’s Clean Fuel Standard (CFS) 
is a statewide initiative to accelerate the use of lower carbon transportation fuels such as biofuel, renewable 
diesel, electricity used to charge electric vehicles, and sustainable aviation fuel. It is designed to reduce 
emissions, strengthen local energy security, and build a more sustainable future for Hawaiʻi.27 

Hawaiʻi paved the way for CFS adoption with the passage of Act 258 in 2026.28 Under this legislation, regulatory 
adoption of CFS is required by January 1, 2028, and implementation by January 1, 2029. Once established, a 
CFS would generate revenue through a credit system: utilities and public agencies that reduce transportation 

FUNDING FREE TRANSIT • 11

emissions by producing low carbon fuels earn credits, and the value of those credits becomes the program’s 
revenue base. 

By design, and legislatively required under Act 258 itself, at least 50 percent of Clean Fuel Standard proceeds 
must go to underserved communities, and eligible investments include electric vehicle rebates, electric 
micromobility, and charging infrastructure.29 

HDOT has identified a CFS as an administrative strategy to incentivise cleaner fuels and reduce transportation 
emissions. HDOT’s Energy Security and Waste Reduction Plan identifies strategies to reduce vehicle miles 
traveled as a key means to meet the state’s climate targets. One of those strategies include using CFS revenue as 
a funding source to make bus transit free for residents 18 and under.30 

•  Advantages: HDOT has already-identified the CFS as a potential funding mechanism to fund fare-free youth 
transit and the department is currently carrying out the administrative groundwork to establish the CFS. 

• 

Trade-offs: HDOT has not yet determined how CFS revenue will be used, or how CFS might support a fare-free 
transit program. Current CFS language shows a bias toward electrification investments rather than transit 
service.31 The implementation timeline doesn’t reach full effect until 2029, and there are no current estimates 
of how much revenue the program would actually generate. 

Option 3: Using a Share of the State Highway Fund

HDOT is the state agency best positioned to fund universal bus transit directly, and the State Highway Fund is 
a potential funding lever available. The State Highway Fund is Hawaiʻi’s dedicated fund for road construction 
and maintenance, built from the state fuel tax, vehicle registration fees, and the state vehicle weight tax. In 2023 
alone, the fund saw revenue of approximately $316 million.32 As estimated in this report, a statewide universal 
fare-free transit program would cost roughly 20 percent of the fund's 2023 revenue.

One targeted way to draw on this fund is to redirect a share of the rental motor vehicle surcharge tax to transit 
before it reaches the State Highway Fund. Rental car companies pay this tax per vehicle per day, and nearly all 
of it is deposited into the fund: about $118 million in FY 2025, up from approximately $106 million in FY 2024, an 
increase of 4.6 percent.33 That growth reflects rate increases, from $3 to $5 per day in 201934 and then in steps to 
$8 per day by 2027.35

HDOT is also legally-bound by the Navahine v. HDOT settlement to decarbonize Hawaiʻi’s transportation sector

Other Funding Sources Worth Exploring

Beyond the three options above, several other jurisdictions have turned to newer, less conventional revenue 
sources that could be examined in future research. This includes: 

•  A heavy or large-vehicle registration fee: Following the District of Columbia’s 2024 restructuring of 

registration fees to charge heavier vehicles more;39 

•  Rideshare trip fees dedicated to transit: An approach several states and cities have adopted and that Hawaiʻi 

itself considered in a 2024 bill (SB3022);40 and 

•  Delivery fees on services like Amazon: A growing number of states are examining this option as a way to 

capture revenue from vehicle trips that gas taxes increasingly miss. 

This report does not model these sources in detail, but they merit further study as the Legislature considers how 
to fund fare-free transit over the long term.

Formula-Based Grants to Counties

Rather than a flat reimbursement of lost fare revenue, this report recommends the state distribute funding to 
counties through a formula-based grant program, adjusted over time as ridership data becomes available. This 
model is based on Washington State’s Transit Support Grant for their own statewide youth ride free program. 
Washington’s program sends each county a set allocation based on population, regardless of exact ridership, 
giving counties several years to collect the ridership data needed to refine the formula going forward.41 

Applied to Hawaiʻi, a grant formula should account for each county’s population, current ridership levels, and 
demonstrated need.

•  Advantages: A grant model gives counties funding certainty, distributes state funds more precisely than 
a uniform per-capita amount, and creates ongoing incentive to collect ridership data, since that data 
determines future funding.

• 

Trade-offs: Designing and administering a formula-based grant program requires more upfront state capacity 
than a flat funding transfer, and counties with weaker current data collection may be disadvantaged in the 
early years unless the state provides transitional support.

HAWAI‘I’S CURRENT 
TRANSIT LANDSCAPE

Public transit in Hawaiʻi is managed by each county independently, producing a fare structure that varies widely 
and inequitably across the state. The result is a system where identical residents pay different prices depending 
solely on which island they call home.

•  Kauaʻi County: No fare-free rider categories; ADA, youth, and senior riders receive a discounted fare.

•  City and County of Honolulu: No fare-free rider categories; youth, seniors, people with disabilities, and low-

income riders receive a reduced fare, however that fare has increased under Bill 54, the most recent fare hike.42

•  Maui County: Free for income-eligible households, youth (under 25), seniors (55+), Medicare and Paratransit 
cardholders, and, as of August 2026, all students and county employees. This leaves only a narrow band of 
non-exempt adult riders still paying a fare.

•  Hawaiʻi County: Fare-free for every rider since 2022. It is the only county in the state with no fare structure at 

all.

•  Statewide: the HIDOE EXPRESS program provides free county bus passes during the school year to eligible 

public school students who live 1.5 miles or more from their home school.

This patchwork has created inequities. Keiki on Oʻahu pay to ride while keiki on Maui ride free. Low-income 
workers on Kauaʻi pay full fare while low-income workers on Maui do not. Kūpuna on the Big Island ride free 
while kūpuna on Oʻahu pay a reduced fare (that just became more expensive under Bill 54).43 Where you live 
should not determine whether you can afford to get around. A statewide universal program would end these 
disparities once and for all.

WHY UNIVERSAL, 
NOT TARGETED?

Our “Keiki Ride Free” report laid out the broad case for fare-free transit: economic, health, safety, and 
environmental benefits well documented by research and Hawaiʻi’s own experience with Hele-On. Rather than 
repeat that case here, this section focuses narrowly on why programs that serve all riders, rather than only 
specific exempted groups, consistently outperformed targeted ones:

• 

Lower administrative costs. A universal program eliminates eligibility requirements entirely. Targeted 
programs, by contrast, require ongoing administration to determine and re-verify who qualifies, which in 
itself is a real and recurring cost.

•  Higher utilization. Removing fares for everyone, not just exempted groups, consistently drives the largest 
ridership gains: fixed-route ridership in Albuquerque grew 49.4 percent in the first year of its universal 
program,44 and daily ridership in Alexandria grew more than 152 percent between 2021 and 2023 after it went 
fare-free for all riders.45 Targeted, exemption based programs do not produce ridership gains of that scale, 
because most riders will still face a fare.

•  No means-testing. A universal program avoids the two-tiered system that targeted exceptions create, where 
some residents must document income, age, or another qualifying status to ride, and others don’t. That 
barrier alone discourages ridership among eligible residents who don’t apply, whether because of stigma, 
paperwork, or simply not knowing the program exists.

•  Meeting state climate goals. State law requires Hawaiʻi to cut transportation emissions in half from 2005 
levels by 2030 and to reach net-negative emissions by 2045.46 To get there, the Hawaiʻi Department of 
Transportation calls for a 20 percent reduction in vehicle miles traveled by 2045.47 Its Energy Security and 
Waste Reduction Plan already names fare-free bus access for youth as one strategy toward that target.48 A 
universal program would go further. It removes the fare for the adults who make most car trips, which is 
where the largest cuts in vehicle miles and emissions are.

Taken together, these are the same reasons Hele-On’s fully open, no-exceptions model has been both effective 
and comparatively simple to administer since 2022 and why we recommend the same approach statewide.

RECOMMENDATIONS

This report recommends that the Hawaiʻi State Legislature:

1.  Enact statewide legislation establishing a permanent, universal, fare-free bus transit program for all four 

county bus systems, with no pass, enrollment, or means-testing, following the model of Hawaiʻi Island’s Hele-
On and Albuquerque’s ABQ Ride Zero Fares.

2.  Fund the program through the barrel tax, the Clean Fuel Standard, the State Highway Fund, or some 

combination of these sources. In practice, this likely means a phased approach. A share of the State Highway 
Fund or barrel tax revenue—both already established mechanisms—could fund a near-term expansion 
along the lines of Keiki Ride Free,49 with Clean Fuel Standard or other revenue layered in as additional, 
complementary sources as the program scales toward a full universal system over the long term. 

3.  Direct that funding to counties through a formula-based grant, modeled on Washington State’s Transit 

Support Grant, tied to population, ridership, and demonstrated need.

4.  Require annual reporting from county transit agencies on ridership, costs, and program outcomes, to inform 

legislative oversight and refind the grant formula over time.

5. 

Invest in service improvements alongside fare elimination. This includes more frequent service and added 
capacity on high-demand routes to meet the ridership growth fare elimination is expected to produce.
